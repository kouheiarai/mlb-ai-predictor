# Seasonal series — shuntō (Japan's spring wage negotiations)

Today is {{TODAY}}. Write this week's instalment of the shuntō series.

## Why this series exists

Shuntō is the coordinated annual round of wage negotiations between Japanese labour unions and
large employers, running from roughly January to April, with the first major settlements announced
in mid-March and Rengō (the national trade union confederation) publishing tallies in rounds
thereafter.

For thirty years it produced almost nothing. That is why it was ignored. It now determines whether
Japan's inflation is self-sustaining, which determines what the Bank of Japan does, which determines
the yen, JGB yields, and the value of every yen-denominated asset on earth.

**This is simultaneously the single most consequential recurring event in Japanese macro and the one
with the thinnest English-language coverage.** Global bond desks care about the number; they read a
one-line wire report about it. Your readers get the actual mechanism.

## What to cover, depending on where we are in the season

- **January to February (setup):** what the unions are demanding and how it compares with last year;
  what the employers' federation has signalled; the government's posture; what economists say is
  needed to sustain the BOJ's inflation target.
- **March (first settlements):** the first-round tally, the large-manufacturer settlements as they
  land, and how the number compares with the same round last year. Be precise about which round of
  the tally you are quoting — the first-round figure is systematically higher than the final one
  because large firms settle first, and conflating rounds is the most common error in coverage.
- **March to April (the real story):** small and medium-sized enterprises. They employ roughly seven
  in ten Japanese workers and settle later and lower. The headline number is not the economy-wide
  number, and the gap between them is the most under-reported fact in Japanese macro.
- **Throughout:** the distinction between the total wage increase and the base-pay increase (*base-up*),
  which strips out seniority-based automatic raises. The base-up figure is the one that matters for
  inflation. Report both and be explicit about which is which.
- **Throughout:** real wages. Nominal increases mean nothing if inflation is running ahead of them.
  Get the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's monthly real wage series and say plainly whether
  workers are ahead or behind.

## Sources

Rengō's own published tallies, Keidanren, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's monthly labour
survey, the Bank of Japan's regional reports, and individual company announcements. Rengō publishes
in Japanese first — go there directly rather than waiting for the English wire summary.

## Accuracy

Always state which tally round a number comes from and its publication date. Never compare a
first-round figure from this year with a final figure from last year — that comparison is wrong and
it is the error that most English coverage makes.
