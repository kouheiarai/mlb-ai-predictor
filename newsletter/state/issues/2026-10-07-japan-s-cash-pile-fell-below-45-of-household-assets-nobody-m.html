The Bank of Japan published the flow of funds for the second quarter on 17 September. Japanese households finished June holding ¥2,519 trillion in financial assets, up 11.0% on the year.

Cash and deposits came to 44.9% of that. The share has been above 50% for most of living memory, and this newsletter reported it at 47.2% in August.

Below 45% will be written up as the moment Japan's savers finally moved. It is not. Look at what grew.

Household assets, end-June 2026 Outstanding Share Year on year
Cash and deposits ¥1,132tn 44.9% +0.5%
Insurance and pensions ¥586tn 23.3% +3.2%
Equity ¥486tn 19.3% +47.0%
Investment trusts ¥193tn 7.7% +36.8%
Debt securities ¥38tn 1.5% +15.5%

The numerator did not move

Deposits grew 0.5% over the year. Not fell — grew, from about ¥1,126 trillion to ¥1,132 trillion. Japanese households did not take money out of the bank. They put slightly more in.

Equity holdings grew 47.0%. Investment trusts grew 36.8%.

That is the entire explanation for the falling cash share. The denominator inflated and the numerator sat still, so the ratio fell. Arithmetic, not behaviour.

Could households have bought their way to a 47% increase? In principle. In practice, that would mean purchasing something like ¥155 trillion of shares in twelve months — and in the first quarter of this year, as this newsletter reported in August, households were net sellers of individual equities even while buying funds. People who are net sellers of a thing do not increase their holdings of it by half through buying.

I want to be careful here, because I could not extract the transaction flows from this release — they are published as charts rather than tables. So this is an inference from growth rates, not a decomposition. But no plausible split rescues the rotation story. Prices did this.

A correction

In August this newsletter reported the cash-and-deposit share at 47.2%, from the first-quarter release.

That release put household assets at ¥2,386 trillion as of end-March. The second-quarter release revises that figure to ¥2,406 trillion — twenty trillion yen higher. Against the revised total, the same deposit balance implies a share of roughly 46.8%, not 47.2%.

It is a small difference and it does not change anything that issue argued. I am flagging it because quarterly statistics get revised, almost nobody who quotes them goes back to check, and a newsletter that asks you to trust its numbers should tell you when one of them moves.

Measured consistently on the revised basis, the share went from about 46.8% in March to 44.9% in June. Still a fall. Still almost entirely valuation.

The line nobody reads

While everyone argues about cash versus stocks, look at the second row of that table.

Insurance and pensions: ¥586 trillion. That is more than Japanese households hold in equities, and it is 23.3% of everything they own — a quarter of the pile, sitting in a category that gets discussed almost nowhere.

It grew 3.2% over the year. Not 47%, not 0.5%: 3.2%. It behaves like neither cash nor equity, because much of it is a contractual claim whose value does not swing with the market and does not sit in a bank either.

The conventional frame for Japan is a binary — money is either dead in deposits or alive in the stock market. The actual balance sheet has a third block nearly as large as the first, and it has been quietly compounding at low single digits while the argument goes on without it.

Why the number keeps moving

On the two-year view, household assets went from ¥2,197 trillion at the end of September 2024 to ¥2,519 trillion now — up about 15%. Japanese households got meaningfully richer on paper in two years, in a country whose savers are routinely described as stuck.

Paper is the operative word, and it cuts both ways: a balance sheet that grew mostly through valuation is a balance sheet that can shrink the same way, without anybody doing anything.

What a real rotation would look like

It would start with the deposit line going down in absolute yen, not up 0.5%. Households would be withdrawing, not merely failing to add.

That has not happened. What has happened, over the issues this newsletter has covered since August, is narrower and more consistent: money leaving the ordinary current account for time deposits and government bonds, which is a response to interest rates returning rather than an appetite for risk; and a tax-free wrapper being widened to admit bond funds, which is the government responding to the same preference.

Meanwhile the equity and fund holdings that Japanese households already owned went up a great deal in price, and the ratio everyone quotes moved as a result.

There is a symmetry worth holding onto. A share that fell because asset prices rose will rise again if asset prices fall, without a single household deciding anything. The 44.9% is a fact about markets at least as much as it is a fact about savers.

One more line from the same release, since this newsletter spent last month on the other side of the ledger: household liabilities now stand at ¥416 trillion, of which ¥397 trillion is borrowings. Both up slightly on the quarter. That side is not a valuation effect. It is money owed, at a rate the Bank of Japan has now raised twice this year.
