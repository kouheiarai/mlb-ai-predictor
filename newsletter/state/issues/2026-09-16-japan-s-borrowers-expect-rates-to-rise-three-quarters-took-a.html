For five issues this newsletter has asked what Japanese households earn: on deposits, on government bonds, on their own labour. That is one side of a balance sheet. This week, the other side.

Japanese households owe ¥414 trillion, of which ¥392 trillion is borrowings. Against ¥2,386 trillion of financial assets that is not alarming in aggregate. But assets and debts are not held by the same households in the same proportion, and the debt reprices.

In February the Japan Housing Finance Agency published its January 2026 survey of mortgage borrowers — 1,237 people who had recently taken a loan, and separately 1,500 people planning to. Three of its findings sit oddly together.

Among people who just took a mortgage Share
Expect rates to rise over the next year 73.7%
Chose a variable-rate loan 75.0%
Say they do not fully understand their rate risk 52.0%

Read the first two lines again. Nearly three-quarters of these borrowers expect the price of money to go up within twelve months. Nearly three-quarters of them took the loan whose price goes up when the price of money goes up.

The third line is the ministry asking, in effect, whether people know what that would cost them. A majority answered somewhere between "a little unsure" and "not at all".

The easy conclusion is the wrong one

It would be simple to write this up as Japanese borrowers behaving irrationally. I do not think that is what the numbers show.

The variable rate on offer has been running around 1.08%, against roughly 3.29% for the flagship 35-year fixed product, according to mortgage comparison service Mogecheck. That is a gap of about 2.2 percentage points, payable now, every month, in exchange for insurance against a rise that may turn out smaller than that.

Framed that way, taking the variable loan is a calculation, not a failure to do one. Fixed-rate insurance in Japan is expensive because the curve is steep.

What the 52% suggests is narrower and more troubling: that a large share of borrowers are not making the calculation at all. You cannot price insurance you have not estimated the cost of going without.

What the question was actually asking

Take a ¥40m loan over 35 years at 1.08%. The payment is about ¥114,400 a month. Put the rate up by one point, to 2.08%, and it becomes about ¥134,200 — roughly ¥19,700 more each month, or ¥237,000 a year. That is what the survey was asking whether people had worked out.

But here is the part that makes the 52% make sense, and it is a feature of the Japanese product that English coverage almost never explains.

Most Japanese variable mortgages carry two rules. Under the five-year rule, the monthly payment does not change for five years even if the rate does. Under the 125% rule, when it finally resets it cannot rise by more than a quarter.

So a borrower whose rate went up last June has seen no change at all on their statement. Nothing arrived in the post. The payment is identical.

What changed is the split inside it. More of each payment goes to interest, less to principal, and the balance falls more slowly than the schedule says. If rates rose far enough that the fixed payment no longer covered even the month's interest, the shortfall would become unpaid interest added to the loan. On the loan above, that would take a rate somewhere north of 3%.

None of that is hidden — it is in the contract. But it means the cost of a rate rise reaches a Japanese variable borrower with a five-year delay and no notification, which is a fairly good explanation for why half of them have not sat down and worked out the number.

The gap between wanting and doing

The survey's second half is where it gets genuinely strange. The agency asked people who intend to borrow within five years what rate type they would want.

Rate type Intend to take Actually took
Variable 37.0% 75.0%
Fixed for an initial period 32.1% 14.9%
Fixed for the whole term 30.9% 10.1%

Intention says 37%. Behaviour says 75%. A thirty-eight point gap.

These are two different samples, not the same people followed over time, so some of the gap is composition. But a gap that size is not a sampling artefact. Something happens between deciding what you want and sitting down at the bank, and whatever it is, it moves people toward the variable loan.

It is starting to move

The direction, at least, has changed. The variable share among actual borrowers fell 4.0 points from the previous survey. Among people planning to borrow, the all-fixed preference is up 4.6 points since April 2024.

Those are the first moves of that size in years, and they are happening while the policy rate sits at 1.0%. One caveat worth stating: the borrowers surveyed took their loans between April and September 2025, before June's rate decision. The next round will be the first to show what households did once the higher rate was actually in front of them.

Why this belongs in a newsletter about savings

Because it is the same household.

The rate normalisation that finally pays a Japanese saver something also bills a Japanese borrower something, and in a country where the typical household holds both a deposit account and a mortgage, those are not separate stories. A quarter-point on the policy rate moves both sides of the ledger — and, as this newsletter found three weeks ago, banks pass rate rises to depositors slowly.

There is no reason to assume they pass them to borrowers at the same pace.
