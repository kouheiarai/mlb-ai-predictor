On 1 September the yield on Japan's 10-year government bond touched 3%, up six basis points on the day and the first time it has been there since 1996. A year ago it was around half that.

Every wire ran it. All of them framed it the same way: a bond market story, about rate-hike expectations and the fiscal outlook. Which it is.

Nobody asked the question a Japanese household would ask, which is whether any of that 3% is available to them.

It is not. Households do not buy the 10-year at auction. What they can buy is the retail government bond, and its rate is set by a formula that hands them a fraction of the market rate.

The 0.66

Japan sells three retail government bonds, and each takes a different cut of the same base rate:

Product How the rate is set September issue Real, vs 1.9% CPI
Fixed 5-year base − 0.05% 2.06% +0.16%
Variable 10-year base × 0.66 1.87% −0.03%
Fixed 3-year base − 0.03% 1.71% −0.19%
Megabank ordinary deposit bank's discretion 0.40% −1.50%

Look at the fixed five-year line. 2.06% against 1.9% inflation. After a generation in which no safe yen asset cleared inflation, one of them now does — by sixteen basis points, before tax on the interest. Call that a tie rather than a win, and it is still the first tie in thirty years.

Then look at the multiplier on the line below it.

A haircut designed for zero

The variable ten-year product pays the base rate times 0.66. The saver gives up a third of the rate in exchange for a bond whose coupon resets with the market rather than locking in.

For most of the time this product has existed, that trade was invisible. When the base rate was 0.2%, two-thirds of it was 0.13%, and the difference was a rounding error on a number nobody was reading anyway.

At 3%, two-thirds is 1.98%. The haircut is now worth a full percentage point a year — on the product the government markets to households as the flexible, safe, inflation-tracking choice.

The formula did not change. The world it was calibrated for did.

What the 0.66 actually buys

In fairness to the product, the multiplier is not simply a fee. The variable ten-year resets its coupon against the prevailing base rate, so a household buying at 1.87% this month is not fixed there — if rates keep climbing, the coupon follows. The fixed five-year has the opposite shape: better today at 2.06%, and then frozen for five years regardless of what inflation does next.

That is a genuine trade, and for the first time in decades it is a trade worth thinking about. Two years ago both products rounded to nothing and the choice between them was academic. The interesting thing is not that one of them is better. It is that the question has a live answer at all.

What the multiplier does is decide how much of the market's move reaches the person taking the other side of it. At 0.2% that share was a technicality. At 3% it is the difference between clearing inflation comfortably and not clearing it.

The September buyer is trading on August's information

One more wrinkle, and it is the kind of thing that only shows up if you read the schedule rather than the headline.

The rates in that table were set for a subscription window that ran from 6 to 31 August, for bonds issued on 15 September. The 10-year touched 3% on 1 September — after the window closed, before the bonds exist.

So a household buying this month's issue is locked to the rate environment of three weeks ago. Not a scandal, just how a monthly issuance calendar works. But it means the gap between the market and the household is wider right now than the table makes it look, and it will keep reopening every time the market moves inside a subscription window.

What this is really measuring

Set the retail bond next to the bank. The fixed five-year pays 2.06%. The megabank ordinary account, at its highest in about 34 years, pays 0.40%. That is a gap of roughly 1.7 percentage points between the two safest places a Japanese household can put money.

One caution on the plus sign in that table. The +0.16% on the fixed five-year is calculated before tax on the interest, and interest in Japan is taxed. Run the tax through and the margin is thin enough that it may not survive. So the honest statement is not that the retail bond now beats inflation — it is that it has stopped clearly losing to it, which after thirty years is still a different world.

For thirty years that gap was close enough to nothing that the choice did not matter, which is exactly why so few households ever made it. Japanese savers are often described as irrationally attached to cash. It is worth considering the simpler explanation: for most of their adult lives, the alternative paid nothing either, and a habit formed under those conditions is not irrational, just out of date.

It has taken a 30-year high in the bond market to make the arithmetic legible again. And the answer it gives is oddly modest: not "buy bonds", but "the safest thing available has finally stopped losing, by a margin thin enough that tax could take it back".

That is not a stampede. It is a country slowly rediscovering that where you keep money is a question with an answer.
