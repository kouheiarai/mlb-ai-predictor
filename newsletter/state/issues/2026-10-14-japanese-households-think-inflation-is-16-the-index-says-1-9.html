Every quarter the Bank of Japan asks a few thousand ordinary people a question no statistical agency asks: by how much do you think prices have changed over the past year?

The September round came out on Monday. Here is what Japan said.

  • Prices rose over the past year — said by 95.5% of respondents
  • By how much, on average — +16.2% · the untrimmed mean is +16.9%
  • By how much, median answer — +10.0%
  • Answered "10.1% or more" — 44.3% of everyone asked

The consumer price index for August, published by the Statistics Bureau three weeks before the survey closed, put inflation at 1.9%.

Japanese households believe prices rose roughly eight and a half times as fast as the index says they did. Take the median instead of the mean, to strip out the extreme answers, and they still believe it was more than five times.

Before anyone calls this ignorance

It is not a Japanese defect, and I do not think it is a mistake in any useful sense. People notice the prices they meet often — rice, eggs, petrol, the lunch set — and barely register the ones they meet twice a decade. A basket weighted by how often you buy something looks nothing like a basket weighted by how much you spend, which is what the index measures.

So households are not getting the answer wrong. They are answering a different question, accurately. The trouble is what happens when policy treats the two answers as the same thing.

It is not a few cranks

The usual way to dismiss a survey number like 16.2% is to say the mean is being dragged by a handful of extreme answers. Normally that is a fair objection. Here it is not available.

44.3% of respondents — nearly half the sample — answered that prices rose 10.1% or more. Another 30.1% put it between 5.1% and 10%. The tail is not a tail. It is most of the country.

And the Bank already trims: the published mean drops the top and bottom half-percent of answers. Without that trim it reads 16.9% rather than 16.2%, so the extremes are worth seven-tenths of a point in a figure sitting fourteen points above the index.

They think the worst is over

One more pair of numbers, because the direction matters as much as the level.

  • Prices over the past year — believed to be +16.2%
  • Prices over the coming year — expected at +11.8% · median +10.0%

Households expect next year to be gentler than the year they think they just lived through. Both the one-year and five-year readings also fell from the June survey. The gap between what people believe and what the index measures is enormous, and it is closing.

The awkward part

This survey is not a curiosity. It is one of the places the Bank of Japan looks when it talks about inflation expectations — and inflation expectations are the reason it gives for raising rates.

From the September statement, which this newsletter went through line by line two weeks ago: there is a risk of inflation overshooting 2% "given factors such as firms' behavior shifting more toward raising wages and prices and medium- to long-term inflation expectations rising."

Here is what medium- to long-term looks like in the Bank's own survey. The question asks for the average annual rate over the coming five years, not the cumulative change:

  • Five years ahead, mean — +10.1% a year · was +10.8% last quarter
  • Five years ahead, median — +5.0% a year
  • Expecting any rise at all — 86.5%
  • Answered "10.1% or more" for five years running — 22.6%

The target is 2%. The median household expects two and a half times that, every year, for five years. The mean expects five times.

Both numbers moved down this quarter, which is the part the headlines missed.

Which is why the pay rise does not land

The same survey carries a number that should be cause for celebration.

The income diffusion index — the share saying their income rose minus the share saying it fell — came in at −5.4, from −10.7 three months earlier. The Bank's own footnote records it as the highest reading since the survey went to post in September 2006. Twenty years, and Japanese households have never felt better about their pay than they do now.

They still do not feel well off. This newsletter has been circling that contradiction for weeks: nominal wages up 4.7%, real wages positive for seven straight months, and a population that does not act as though any of it happened.

Put the two survey answers side by side and it stops being a contradiction. If you believe prices rose 16% and your pay rose 5%, you have had a large pay cut. The arithmetic in your head is wrong by the index's lights and completely coherent by your own.

What it explains

Run the past two months of this newsletter back through that lens.

  • The megabank deposit at 0.40% — against measured inflation that is a loss of 1.5 points a year · against perceived inflation it is a catastrophe
  • The retail government bond at 2.06% — the first safe yen asset in a generation to clear the index · and nowhere near clearing what people believe
  • Three-quarters of borrowers taking a variable mortgage — rational if you expect inflation to erode the debt faster than the rate rises

Every one of those choices looks more sensible, not less, once you know what the chooser thinks inflation is.

The Bank of Japan has a 2% target, an index printing 1.9%, and a public that thinks the number is 16. It publishes all three itself, every quarter, and the third one is the only one nobody reports.
