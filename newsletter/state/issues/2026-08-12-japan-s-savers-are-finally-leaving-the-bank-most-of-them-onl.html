Imagine leaving your salary in a bank account for twenty-five years and earning, in total, almost nothing. Not a bad year. Not a rough decade. Nothing, as a matter of policy, for an entire working life.

That was the deal for Japanese households, and they took it anyway. Roughly half of the largest private pool of savings on earth just sat in ordinary bank accounts, going quietly nowhere.

That deal is now ending, and last quarter it ended faster than it ever has.

The number that got everyone's attention

The Bank of Japan's flow of funds accounts for January to March showed the biggest net outflow from ordinary deposits ever recorded. Cash and deposits fell to 47.2% of household financial assets, from 47.6% three months earlier. That share sat above 50% for decades and only broke below it last year.

Households are sitting on ¥2,385.7 trillion in total. If that number is hard to picture, try this: it is about three and a half times everything Japan produces in a year. And it grew by ¥158.9 trillion over twelve months.

So far, so tidy. Japan's famously cautious savers are moving into markets, the thirty-year story finally turns, and you have probably read a version of that sentence this summer.

Here is the wrinkle.

Where the money actually went

Smooth the flows over four quarters and households added ¥5.8 trillion. Look at where it landed:

DestinationFour-quarter average flow
Cash and deposits+¥1.7tn
Equities and investment trusts+¥1.5tn (investment trusts alone +¥3.0tn)
Debt securities+¥1.3tn
Insurance and pensions+¥1.3tn

Look at the first row again. Deposits, as a category, are still growing.

What is collapsing is one particular kind — the liquidity deposit, the ordinary account your salary lands in, paying next to nothing. The money walking out of it is mostly walking to the time deposit down the hall, or into a government bond, or into an insurance product. Same building. Different room.

Because after a generation in which every safe asset in Japan paid zero, several of them suddenly pay something. Long-term bank lending rates crossed 2% in June for the first time in twenty-five years. A household moving from a current account into a time deposit has not developed an appetite for risk. It has noticed that interest exists again.

One more detail worth pausing on. Equities and investment trusts together took in ¥1.5 trillion, but investment trusts alone took in ¥3.0 trillion. Japanese households are buying funds and selling individual shares at the same time.

The investment story is real too

None of this makes the other half small. Cumulative buying through NISA — Japan's tax-free investment account, and the government's main lever for prying money out of banks — hit roughly ¥71 trillion by the end of December, up 36% in a year, spread across about 28.3 million accounts. The ¥56 trillion target set for 2027 was cleared back in March 2025.

Dai-ichi Life's economists also flag reports that the government wants equities, funds and bonds to reach 40% of household assets by fiscal 2040. They are at 25.2% today. Treat that as reported intent rather than settled policy — but nobody is being coy about the direction.

Why this is hard to see from outside Japan

Three things, and all of them live in the Japanese tables.

The first is that deposit distinction. English coverage collapses everything into "deposits." The Japanese statistics keep liquidity deposits and time deposits apart, and the whole story of this quarter is the gap between them. Working from the English summary, you would see total deposits tick up and conclude nothing had happened.

The second is that this release quietly revised history. The end-2025 household total was previously reported around ¥2,351 trillion and now reads ¥2,394.1 trillion. If you compare a fresh figure against an article written in the spring, you are comparing two different series and will get a nonsense answer.

The third barely got reported at all: on a seasonally adjusted basis, households became net borrowers last quarter, at −¥1.3 trillion against +¥4.9 trillion in October–December. Japanese households have been a dependable surplus sector for decades. Dai-ichi Life is careful about it — this series swings hard once you seasonally adjust it, and the four-quarter average is still comfortably in surplus — but it is worth a pin in the map.

What to watch

The next release covers April to June and lands in September. Dai-ichi Life expects a fresh record for household assets on the back of the equity rally — worth remembering that is a valuation effect, not people putting money in.

Underneath sits a quieter problem. The BOJ now owns 42.21% of Japanese government bonds, down from 43.10% and falling for the ninth quarter running. Someone has to hold the paper the central bank has stopped buying, and Japanese households are being courted for the job. Improvements to retail JGB terms have been trailed; watch whether they actually arrive.

And watch the ratio rather than the headline. If the cash share keeps sliding while fund inflows flatten out, the money is just circulating inside the safe bucket — and "savings to investment" is a weaker signal than it looks.
