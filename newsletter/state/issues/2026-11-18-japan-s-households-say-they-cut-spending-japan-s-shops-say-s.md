# Japan's households say they cut spending. Japan's shops say sales rose 4%.

*The Cabinet Office publishes both numbers. Last quarter they disagreed by 3.1 trillion yen, the widest gap since 1994.*

- 配信日: 2026-11-18
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**

7-9月期GDP（11月16日公表）は年率マイナス0.1%で4四半期ぶりの減少となり、原因は個人消費とされた。だが同じ公表物の参考系列には家計消費の推計値が2つ載っていて、家計側（需要側）はマイナス0.9%、企業側（供給側）はプラス4.4%と逆方向を向いている。差は3.1兆円、伸び率の差は5.3ポイントで、1994年までさかのぼる系列で最大。さらに家計調査の落ち込みのうち教育費と光熱費は、高校授業料無償化と電気・ガス料金支援という政策の効果が相当部分を占める。

**確信度が低い箇所**

- 経済産業省の2026年6月12日公表ページは自動取得を拒否する（403）ため、本文を直接読めていない。値引き単価（3.5／4.5／3.5円/kWh）は meti.go.jp に限定した検索結果が同公表物と令和8年度予備費資料を引用したもので、2つの資料で一致している。ただし一次資料の原文を目で確認できていないので、公開前に1度ブラウザで開いて単価を確かめてほしい。2025年夏の単価（2.0〜2.4円/kWh）はさらに根拠が弱いため、本文では「2025年の夏の制度の単価はこれより低かった」とだけ書き、倍率を断定していない。
- 光熱・水道の落ち込みのうち、どれだけが値引き拡大によるものかは計算していない。上下水道料も下がっているので補助だけでは説明できない。本文でもそう書いてある。
- 需要側・供給側の推計値は名目・原系列で、公表されているGDPの実質・季調値とは直接比較できない。本文では「名目・原系列」と明示している。
- 「公表値は2つを混ぜたもの」という説明は、内閣府が並行推計項目として両方を公表し共通推計項目と合わせて推計していることに基づく一般的な理解。どの四半期にどちらをどの比率で採用したかは公表されていないため、本文では配分には踏み込んでいない。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**

個別の銘柄・ファンド・通貨への言及なし。価格予想なし。「買うべき」「安い」「高い」に類する表現なし。金利や消費の先行きについて筆者の予想は書いていない。日銀の政策判断については「この統計を材料のひとつにしている」という事実関係のみで、次回会合の予想は書いていない。マネーストックの記述は統計上の動きの説明にとどめ、預金商品を勧める書き方は避けた。

## 本文（この HTML を beehiiv に貼る）

```html
<p>Every English write-up of Japan's third-quarter GDP said the same thing, and it was a fair reading of the headline: the economy shrank because households stopped spending. I went into the Cabinet Office's own spreadsheets to see how much they had stopped, and found two answers.</p>

<p>Attached to the same release, in a reference table almost nobody opens, are two estimates of what households spent last quarter. One is built from what households report. The other from what shops and service firms report. Last quarter the first fell and the second rose, and the distance between them is the widest in the thirty-two years the series covers.</p>

<h2>The short version</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Real GDP fell at an annualised <strong>0.1%</strong> in July to September, the first contraction in four quarters, and household consumption did the shrinking.</li>
  <li>By households' own numbers, they spent <strong>0.9% less</strong> in yen than a year earlier. By shops' numbers, <strong>4.4% more</strong>.</li>
  <li>The two estimates sit <strong>&yen;3.1 trillion</strong> apart in one quarter &mdash; about one yen in twelve, and the biggest gap since the series starts in 1994.</li>
  <li>All of it happened while real employee compensation rose <strong>2.4%</strong> on the year. Whatever households did, they did it while getting better off.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What the headline says</h2>

<p><strong>The economy shrank by roughly a rounding error, and consumption is why.</strong></p>

<p>From the <a href="https://www.esri.cao.go.jp/jp/sna/data/data_list/sokuhou/files/2026/qe263/gdemenuja.html">first preliminary estimate</a> of 16 November, seasonally adjusted:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Real GDP</strong> &mdash; &minus;0.02% on the quarter &middot; <strong>&minus;0.1% annualised</strong> &middot; first fall since Q3 2025</li>
  <li><strong>Household consumption</strong> &mdash; &minus;0.3% &middot; &minus;1.3% annualised &middot; second consecutive fall</li>
  <li><strong>Durable goods</strong> &mdash; <strong>&minus;3.5%</strong> &middot; &minus;13.2% annualised</li>
  <li><strong>Services</strong> &mdash; +0.1% &middot; +0.3% annualised</li>
  <li><strong>Real compensation of employees</strong> &mdash; <strong>+2.4%</strong> on the year</li>
</ul>

<p>Here is the part that trips everyone up, including me. In yen, households spent <em>more</em>: nominal consumption rose 0.4% on the quarter, from &yen;348.6 trillion to &yen;350.0 trillion at an annual rate. The whole real decline is the price deflator. Households handed over more money and came home with less.</p>

<h2>Two numbers for the same thing, in the same release</h2>

<p><strong>The Cabinet Office cannot tell you exactly what households spent, and it admits so in public every quarter.</strong></p>

<p>Japan's national accounts build household consumption twice. The <em>demand-side estimate</em> comes from surveys of households, what people say they bought. The <em>supply-side estimate</em> comes from retail and services statistics, what businesses say they sold. The published GDP figure draws on both, and the two raw estimates go out side by side in a <a href="https://www.esri.cao.go.jp/jp/sna/data/data_list/sokuhou/files/2026/qe263/tables/jkksuikei.csv">reference file</a>, in nominal terms, so anyone can see how far apart they are.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Demand-side estimate</strong> &mdash; &yen;37.4tn for the quarter &middot; <strong>&minus;0.9%</strong> on the year</li>
  <li><strong>Supply-side estimate</strong> &mdash; &yen;40.5tn &middot; <strong>+4.4%</strong> on the year</li>
  <li><strong>The gap</strong> &mdash; <strong>&yen;3.1tn</strong> &middot; 8.3% of the demand-side figure</li>
  <li><strong>Gap between the growth rates</strong> &mdash; <strong>5.3 percentage points</strong> &middot; widest since the series begins in Q1 1994</li>
  <li><strong>Previous widest</strong> &mdash; 4.1 points, in Q1 2009</li>
</ul>

<p>Annualised, that disagreement runs at roughly &yen;12 trillion a year against a nominal economy of &yen;693 trillion. Call it 1.8% of GDP that nobody can locate.</p>

<p>I cannot tell you which one is right, and neither can the Cabinet Office &mdash; that is why it publishes both. What I can say is that the gap is at a record, one series fell while the other rose, and Monday's headline sits on top of that.</p>

<h2>Part of the fall is the government paying the bill</h2>

<p><strong>Two of the deepest cuts in the household survey are policy, not thrift.</strong></p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.stat.go.jp/data/kakei/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/fies_mr.pdf">Family Income and Expenditure Survey</a> for September, out on 10 November, is the gloomiest of the lot: &yen;289,536 per household, down <strong>6.8%</strong> in real terms, a tenth consecutive monthly fall, every category negative, and 4.5% down on a three-month average. Then look at which categories led it.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Education</strong> &mdash; <strong>&minus;23.5%</strong> in real terms on the year</li>
  <li><strong>Utilities</strong> &mdash; <strong>&minus;10.5%</strong> &middot; seventh straight fall</li>
  <li><strong>Transport and communication</strong> &mdash; &minus;9.1% &middot; the largest single drag</li>
  <li><strong>Recreation</strong> &mdash; &minus;8.7%</li>
  <li><strong>Food</strong> &mdash; &minus;2.5% &middot; fourth straight fall</li>
</ul>

<p>On 1 April 2026 Japan abolished the income cap on high-school tuition support and raised the private-school ceiling to <a href="https://www.mext.go.jp/a_menu/shotou/mushouka/index.htm">&yen;457,200 a year</a>, from &yen;396,000. The money goes to the school, not the parent, so it never shows up as household income and it does show up as a collapse in household education spending. In this survey, a family whose tuition bill was just cancelled looks exactly like a family that gave up on school.</p>

<p>Utilities work the same way. The government <a href="https://www.meti.go.jp/press/2026/06/20260612003/20260612003.html">discounted electricity</a> for July, August and September usage at &yen;3.5, &yen;4.5 and &yen;3.5 per kilowatt-hour, straight onto the bill, no application. The 2025 summer discount was smaller, so part of this year's fall is the subsidy growing rather than households using less. Only part: water and sewerage fell too, and no subsidy touched those.</p>

<p>So where did the money go? Toward the longer-dated end of the bank: in the Bank of Japan's <a href="https://www.boj.or.jp/en/statistics/money/ms/ms2610.pdf">October money stock</a>, time and savings deposits were up 4.4% while demand deposits shrank 0.2% and cash in circulation shrank 1.5%. That series counts companies too, so read it as a direction, not a household number.</p>

<p><strong>72.7%</strong> &mdash; the share of take-home pay Japanese worker households spent in September, against 82.1% a year earlier.</p>

<h2>Why this is hard to see from outside Japan</h2>

<ol>
  <li>The two parallel estimates live in a Japanese-only reference CSV attached to the release. No English version, and no press line pointing at it.</li>
  <li>The tuition change took effect on 1 April 2026. It lands as a fall in household spending rather than a rise in household income, and no English coverage of the GDP number mentioned it.</li>
  <li>The energy discount is applied by the utility before the bill is issued. Households never see a transfer, and the statistics never record one.</li>
  <li>Japan runs two official measures of household spending. For September the household survey said &minus;6.8% on the year and the national accounts said &minus;0.3%. English coverage quotes whichever it found first.</li>
  <li>The <a href="https://www.mhlw.go.jp/toukei/itiran/roudou/monthly/r08/2609p/dl/pdf2609p.pdf">Monthly Labour Survey</a> gives two real-wage figures for September, deflated two ways: +2.2% and +2.3%. And the 4.6% rise in total cash earnings was flattered by special payments, up 32.6%; scheduled pay, the clean number, rose 3.7%.</li>
</ol>

<h2>What to watch</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Friday 20 November, 8:30am Tokyo</strong> &mdash; October national CPI. September ran at +2.1%. This is the deflator that decides whether real wages stay positive for a tenth month.</li>
  <li><strong>The second estimate of third-quarter GDP, due in December</strong> &mdash; and whether the revision moves toward the supply-side figure or the demand-side one.</li>
  <li><strong>October's household survey</strong> &mdash; the energy discount covered usage through September, so October is the first month without it. If utilities stop falling, we learn how much of the slump was the subsidy.</li>
</ul>

<p>What I keep turning over is this. The Bank of Japan reads this consumption signal when it sets rates, and the signal is assembled from two sources &yen;3.1 trillion apart. If you remember one thing, make it that Monday's headline was a blend of a number that fell and a number that rose. If you know a sharper way to tell which of the two is lying, hit reply.</p>
```
