The Bank of Japan met on 29 and 30 October and left the policy rate at 1.25%. On the face of it, nothing happened.

Three things happened.

What changed in the language

The October Outlook Report carries the Bank's assessment of where inflation actually is. Set it against the September statement this newsletter went through five weeks ago.

  • Underlying inflation, September — "underlying CPI inflation has been approaching 2 percent"
  • Underlying inflation, October — "underlying CPI inflation is at a level that is generally consistent with the price stability target of 2 percent"
  • The supporting footnote, new in October — across indicators for core CPI, inflation expectations and trend inflation, "many of these indicators are at around 2 percent"

Approaching became arrived. After roughly three decades of trying, the Bank is saying the 2% target is met.

And then it did not raise rates.

The dissent changed sides

September's vote was 7–2, both dissenters arguing the Bank should not have raised. October's was 8–1, and the one holdout wanted the opposite.

  • Asada and Sato — dissented in September against raising · voted with the majority in October
  • Takata — voted for the September hike · dissented in October, formally proposing 1.5%

His stated reason is the interesting part: with underlying inflation having reached the target, the Bank has entered a new phase in which it should move faster toward a neutral rate that leaves room to respond. His proposal was voted down.

So a board member read the Bank's own new sentence and concluded it implied a faster path. The majority read the same sentence and held.

Worth a caveat: the motion changed too. Voting against a rate rise in September and for a hold in October is not necessarily a change of mind. But Takata's is, and he spelled it out.

The equity selling doubled

This passed with no dissent at all, and it is the decision with the largest number attached.

The Bank spent years buying equity ETFs and now holds a very large position it has been unwinding since January at a deliberately unremarkable pace. From January 2027 that pace rises by about two and a half times:

  • Old pace — about ¥330bn a year at book value · roughly 0.05% of market turnover
  • New pace — about ¥860bn a year at book value · roughly 0.1% of turnover
  • At market value — the Bank's own footnote puts the new pace at about ¥2.46 trillion a year
  • J-REITs — unchanged at about ¥5bn a year

The Bank's reasoning is procedural: the selling has gone smoothly, trading volumes have grown, so the same share of the market now supports a larger number. Nobody voted against it.

Two issues ago this newsletter found that household equity holdings grew 47% in a year on valuation rather than buying. The largest single holder of Japanese equities has now decided to sell into that market at about two and a half times the previous rate.

The forecast, and the line underneath it

Medians of the board's projections, with July's in brackets:

  • FY2026 — GDP +0.7% [+0.6%] · core CPI +2.5% [+2.5%]
  • FY2027 — GDP +0.7% [+0.8%] · core CPI +2.6% [+2.4%]
  • FY2028 — GDP +0.8% [+0.8%] · core CPI +2.0% [+2.0%]

Three consecutive years at or above target, with FY2027 revised up two-tenths. Risks to the CPI outlook are described as skewed to the upside.

Now the footnote, which is where the actual uncertainty lives. The projections exclude a temporary cut to the consumption tax on food and drink currently before the Diet. If it passes as drafted, the Bank estimates it would take about 1.3 percentage points off FY2027 core CPI.

That would put the central forecast at roughly 1.3% — well under target — through a tax change rather than anything monetary. The Bank has declared the target met using a number a bill in parliament could halve.

What the hold means downstairs

For the household balance sheet this newsletter has been tracking, a hold means the recent past continues.

  • The megabank ordinary deposit — 0.40%, set when the policy rate was 1.0% · the banks never passed through the September rise, and now there is nothing new to pass through
  • The retail government bond — priced off a base rate that has stopped moving · the variable ten-year still hands over two-thirds of it
  • Variable-rate mortgages — three-quarters of recent borrowers · the five-year rule means they would not have felt a rise anyway, and now there is not one

The one thing that did change is the only one households do not transact in directly. The Bank is selling more equities, and the equity block of the household balance sheet is the part that has been doing all the growing.

One more number, from the same bank

The Outlook says medium- to long-term inflation expectations "are at around 2 percent."

Seventeen days earlier the Bank published its household survey, which this newsletter covered last issue. Asked what prices would do over the next five years, households answered 10.1% a year on the mean and 5.0% on the median.

Both numbers are the Bank's own, and both are honest. Expectations are measured across markets, firms and households, and the footnote says many indicators sit near 2%. Households are simply not one of the ones that does.

Which is a reasonable way to run a central bank, and a strange thing to be true of a country. The institution setting the price of money believes inflation is 2%. The people holding ¥1,132 trillion of deposits believe it is five times that. Policy is being set against the first number. Saving is being decided by the second.
