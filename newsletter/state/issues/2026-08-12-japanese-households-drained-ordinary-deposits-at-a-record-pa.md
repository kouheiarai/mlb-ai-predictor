# Japanese households drained ordinary deposits at a record pace last quarter

*The cash share of household wealth fell to 47.2%. Most of what left never went near a stock.*

- 配信日: 2026-08-12
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**
2026年1-3月期の資金循環統計で、家計の流動性預金からの純流出が過去最大になった。ただしその資金の多くは株式や投資信託ではなく、定期預金・個人向け国債・保険年金といった「安全資産の中での移動」に向かっている。海外メディアは現預金比率の低下をリスク選好の高まりとして報じがちだが、実態の相当部分は金利が復活したことによる資産内の裁定であり、性質が違う。

**使った数字と出典の対応**
- 2,385.7兆円 — 2026年3月末の家計金融資産残高 — 日銀 資金循環統計 2026年1-3月期速報（第一生命経済研究所レポート経由、2026-06公表）
- 2,394.1兆円 — 前期（2025年12月末）残高。過去最高 — 同上
- +158.9兆円（ニッセイは159兆円と表記） — 前年同期差 — ニッセイ基礎研究所
- 47.2%（前期47.6%） — 家計金融資産に占める現預金比率 — 第一生命経済研究所
- 25.2%（前期24.6%） — 株式・投資信託・債券の比率 — 同上
- +5.8兆円 — 家計の資産側フロー4四半期移動平均。内訳は現預金+1.7、株式等・投信+1.5（うち投信+3.0）、債務証券+1.3、保険年金+1.3 — 同上
- ▲1.3兆円 — 家計の資金過不足（季調値）。前期+4.9兆円から資金不足に転じた — 同上
- 「流動性預金の純流出は過去最大」「投信の純流入は過去2番目」 — ニッセイ基礎研究所
- 42.21%（前期43.10%）、9四半期連続低下 — 日銀の国債保有比率 — 第一生命経済研究所
- 約71兆円（前年比+36%）、約2,826万口座（+10%） — NISA累計買付額と口座数、2025年12月末時点の速報値 — 金融庁（2026-02-18公表、日経報道経由）

**確信度が低い箇所**
3点ある。
1. 2025年12月末の残高について、ニッセイの前回レポートは2,351兆円としているが、今回の速報は遡及改定を含んでおり、第一生命は前期を2,394.1兆円としている。本文は改定後の数字を採用した。改定の詳細は日銀の原資料まで当たれていない。
2. NISAの数値は金融庁の公表を日経報道経由で取得した。金融庁の該当ページ自体は取得できず、内訳（つみたて枠と成長枠の別、商品別）は確認できていないため本文でも触れていない。
3. 「2040年度までに株式・投資信託・債券を4割へ」は第一生命レポートが「報じられている」と記載しているもので、確定した政府目標ではない。本文でも報道ベースであることを明示した。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**
個別銘柄・ファンド・通貨への言及なし。価格予想なし。「買うべき」に類する表現なし。将来見通しは第一生命の見解（次回統計で過去最高更新）として発言者に帰属させた。政府目標は報道ベースであることを明示。問題は認められない。

## 本文（この HTML を beehiiv に貼る）

```html
<p>Japanese households pulled money out of ordinary bank deposits faster than at any point on record last quarter. That is the headline from the Bank of Japan's <a href="https://www.boj.or.jp/statistics/sj/index.htm">flow of funds accounts</a> for January to March 2026, and it is the kind of number that gets written up abroad as proof that Japan's savers have finally discovered risk.</p>

<p>They have, partly. But if you follow where the money actually went, most of it never came close to a stock.</p>

<h2>The numbers first</h2>

<p>Household financial assets stood at ¥2,385.7 trillion at the end of March, down slightly from ¥2,394.1 trillion three months earlier but ¥158.9 trillion higher than a year before, <a href="https://www.dlri.co.jp/report/macro/623451.html">according to Dai-ichi Life Research Institute's reading</a> of the release.</p>

<p>The share held in cash and deposits fell to <strong>47.2%</strong>, from 47.6% the previous quarter. That ratio sat comfortably above 50% for decades and broke below it only last year. Equities, investment trusts and bonds together rose to 25.2% from 24.6%.</p>

<p>Underneath, <a href="https://www.nli-research.co.jp/report/detail/id=86062?site=nli">NLI Research Institute notes</a> two records in the same table: the net outflow from liquidity deposits — ordinary, instant-access accounts — was the largest ever recorded, and the net inflow into investment trusts was the second largest.</p>

<h2>Where it went is the interesting part</h2>

<p>Smoothed over four quarters, households added ¥5.8 trillion of financial assets in the quarter. The breakdown does not look like a stampede into equities:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Destination</th><th>Four-quarter average flow</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Cash and deposits</td><td>+¥1.7tn</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Equities and investment trusts</td><td>+¥1.5tn (investment trusts alone +¥3.0tn)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Debt securities</td><td>+¥1.3tn</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Insurance and pensions</td><td>+¥1.3tn</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Read that first row again. <strong>Deposits as a category are still growing.</strong> What is collapsing is one specific kind of deposit — the zero-interest current account — while time deposits, government bonds and insurance products absorb the money leaving it.</p>

<p>Note also that equities and investment trusts net to +¥1.5 trillion while investment trusts alone take in +¥3.0 trillion. Households are buying funds and selling individual shares at the same time.</p>

<p>This matters because "Japan's savers are moving from savings to investment" is doing two jobs at once, and only one of them is about appetite for risk. The other is plain arbitrage: after a generation in which every safe asset in Japan paid nothing, several of them now pay something. Long-term bank lending rates crossed 2% in June for the first time in 25 years. A household shifting from an ordinary account to a time deposit or a retail JGB has not become an investor. It has noticed that interest exists again.</p>

<h2>The NISA channel is real, and large</h2>

<p>None of which makes the investment story small. Cumulative purchases through NISA, Japan's tax-advantaged retail investment account, reached roughly ¥71 trillion by the end of December 2025, up 36% in a year, across about 28.3 million accounts, according to Financial Services Agency figures <a href="https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGXZQOUB188MA0Y6A210C2000000/">reported by Nikkei</a>. The government's ¥56 trillion target for 2027 was cleared in March 2025; the account target of 34 million is about 80% met.</p>

<p>Dai-ichi Life also notes reports that the government intends to set a target of lifting equities, investment trusts and bonds to 40% of household financial assets by fiscal 2040 — against 25.2% today. Treat that as reported intent rather than settled policy, but the direction of travel is not ambiguous.</p>

<h2>Why this is hard to see from outside Japan</h2>

<p>Three things get lost in translation, and all three are visible only in the Japanese tables.</p>

<p>The first is the deposit distinction. English summaries collapse everything into "deposits." The Japanese statistics separate <em>liquidity</em> deposits from <em>time</em> deposits, and the entire story of this quarter lives in the gap between them. A reader working from the English release would see a modest rise in total deposits and conclude nothing happened.</p>

<p>The second is that this release carried retroactive revisions. The end-2025 household total was previously reported around ¥2,351 trillion and now reads ¥2,394.1 trillion. Anyone comparing a fresh figure against an older write-up is comparing two different series.</p>

<p>The third is a genuine turn that went almost unremarked: on a seasonally adjusted basis, <strong>households moved into net financial deficit in the quarter</strong>, at −¥1.3 trillion against +¥4.9 trillion in October–December. Japanese households have been a reliable surplus sector for decades. Dai-ichi Life is cautious about it — seasonally adjusted figures for this series swing hard, and the four-quarter average still shows a surplus — but it is worth a marker.</p>

<h2>What to watch</h2>

<p>The next flow of funds release covers April to June and lands in September. Dai-ichi Life expects it to set a fresh record for household assets, on the strength of the equity rally through June — a valuation effect, not a flow.</p>

<p>Two other things sit underneath. The BOJ's ownership share of Japanese government bonds fell to 42.21% from 43.10%, a ninth consecutive quarterly decline; somebody has to hold the paper the central bank is no longer buying, and Japanese households are being actively courted as part of the answer. Watch whether the promised improvements to retail JGB terms actually appear.</p>

<p>And watch the ratio, not the headline. If the cash share keeps falling while investment trust inflows plateau, the money is moving inside the safe bucket, and the "savings to investment" narrative is a weaker signal than it looks.</p>
```
