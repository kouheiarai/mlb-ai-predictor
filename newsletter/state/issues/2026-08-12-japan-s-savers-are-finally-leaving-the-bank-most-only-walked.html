Imagine leaving your salary in a bank account for twenty-five years and earning, in total, almost nothing. Not a bad year. Not a rough decade. Nothing, as a matter of policy, for an entire working life.

That was the deal for Japanese households, and they took it anyway. Last quarter, they walked away from it faster than they ever have.

The short version

  • Cash and deposits fell to 47.2% of household financial assets. That share sat above 50% for decades.
  • Ordinary deposits saw their largest net outflow on record.
  • But deposits as a whole are still growing. Most of the money moved to time deposits and bonds — not to stocks.
  • The reason is boring and enormous: after a generation at zero, safe assets in Japan pay interest again.

The headline number

Japanese households hold ¥2,385.7 trillion — about three and a half times everything Japan produces in a year.

The Bank of Japan's flow of funds accounts for January to March:

  • Cash and deposits: 47.2% of the total, down from 47.6%
  • Equities, funds and bonds: 25.2%, up from 24.6%
  • Total assets grew ¥158.9 trillion over twelve months

So far, so tidy. Japan's cautious savers are moving into markets, the thirty-year story finally turns, and you have probably read a version of that sentence this summer.

Where the money actually went

Out of the current account — and mostly into another safe asset down the hall.

Smooth the flows over four quarters and households added ¥5.8 trillion:

DestinationFour-quarter average flow
Cash and deposits+¥1.7tn
Equities and investment trusts+¥1.5tn (funds alone +¥3.0tn)
Debt securities+¥1.3tn
Insurance and pensions+¥1.3tn

Look at the first row. Deposits as a category are still growing. What is collapsing is one particular kind — the liquidity deposit, the ordinary account your salary lands in, paying next to nothing.

Same building. Different room.

Long-term bank lending rates crossed 2% in June, a twenty-five-year high. A household moving from a current account to a time deposit has not developed an appetite for risk. It has noticed that interest exists again.

The line nobody is quoting

Equities and funds together took in ¥1.5 trillion. Funds alone took in ¥3.0 trillion.

Japanese households are buying investment trusts and net selling individual shares at the same time. The "retail is coming for Japanese equities" story is, at the household level, mostly a story about funds.

The fund channel is genuinely large: cumulative buying through NISA — Japan's tax-free investment account — reached roughly ¥71 trillion across about 28.3 million accounts by end-December, up 36% in a year. The ¥56 trillion target set for 2027 was cleared in March 2025. Dai-ichi Life's economists also flag reports that the government wants equities, funds and bonds at 40% of household assets by fiscal 2040, against 25.2% now — reported intent rather than settled policy, but nobody is being coy about the direction.

Why this is hard to see from outside Japan

  1. English coverage says "deposits." The Japanese tables say two different things. They separate liquidity deposits from time deposits, and this quarter's entire story is the gap between them. From the English summary you would see total deposits tick up and conclude nothing happened.
  2. This release quietly revised history. The end-2025 household total was previously reported around ¥2,351 trillion and now reads ¥2,394.1 trillion. Compare a fresh figure against a spring article and you are comparing two different series.
  3. Households became net borrowers and almost nobody reported it. Seasonally adjusted, the household balance went to −¥1.3 trillion from +¥4.9 trillion. They have been a dependable surplus sector for decades. Dai-ichi Life is careful — this series swings hard once adjusted, and the four-quarter average is still in surplus — but it is worth a pin in the map.

What to watch

  • September: the April–June release. Dai-ichi Life expects a record for household assets on the equity rally — a valuation effect, not people putting money in.
  • Retail JGBs: the BOJ now owns 42.21% of Japanese government bonds, down for a ninth straight quarter. Someone has to hold what the central bank stopped buying, and households are being courted for the job. Improvements to retail JGB terms have been trailed; watch whether they arrive.
  • The ratio, not the headline: if the cash share keeps sliding while fund inflows flatten, the money is just circulating inside the safe bucket — and "savings to investment" is a weaker signal than it looks.
