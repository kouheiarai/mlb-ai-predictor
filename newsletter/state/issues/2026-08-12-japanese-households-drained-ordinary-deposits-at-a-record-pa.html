Japanese households pulled money out of ordinary bank deposits faster than at any point on record last quarter. That is the headline from the Bank of Japan's flow of funds accounts for January to March 2026, and it is the kind of number that gets written up abroad as proof that Japan's savers have finally discovered risk.

They have, partly. But if you follow where the money actually went, most of it never came close to a stock.

The numbers first

Household financial assets stood at ¥2,385.7 trillion at the end of March, down slightly from ¥2,394.1 trillion three months earlier but ¥158.9 trillion higher than a year before, according to Dai-ichi Life Research Institute's reading of the release.

The share held in cash and deposits fell to 47.2%, from 47.6% the previous quarter. That ratio sat comfortably above 50% for decades and broke below it only last year. Equities, investment trusts and bonds together rose to 25.2% from 24.6%.

Underneath, NLI Research Institute notes two records in the same table: the net outflow from liquidity deposits — ordinary, instant-access accounts — was the largest ever recorded, and the net inflow into investment trusts was the second largest.

Where it went is the interesting part

Smoothed over four quarters, households added ¥5.8 trillion of financial assets in the quarter. The breakdown does not look like a stampede into equities:

DestinationFour-quarter average flow
Cash and deposits+¥1.7tn
Equities and investment trusts+¥1.5tn (investment trusts alone +¥3.0tn)
Debt securities+¥1.3tn
Insurance and pensions+¥1.3tn

Read that first row again. Deposits as a category are still growing. What is collapsing is one specific kind of deposit — the zero-interest current account — while time deposits, government bonds and insurance products absorb the money leaving it.

Note also that equities and investment trusts net to +¥1.5 trillion while investment trusts alone take in +¥3.0 trillion. Households are buying funds and selling individual shares at the same time.

This matters because "Japan's savers are moving from savings to investment" is doing two jobs at once, and only one of them is about appetite for risk. The other is plain arbitrage: after a generation in which every safe asset in Japan paid nothing, several of them now pay something. Long-term bank lending rates crossed 2% in June for the first time in 25 years. A household shifting from an ordinary account to a time deposit or a retail JGB has not become an investor. It has noticed that interest exists again.

The NISA channel is real, and large

None of which makes the investment story small. Cumulative purchases through NISA, Japan's tax-advantaged retail investment account, reached roughly ¥71 trillion by the end of December 2025, up 36% in a year, across about 28.3 million accounts, according to Financial Services Agency figures reported by Nikkei. The government's ¥56 trillion target for 2027 was cleared in March 2025; the account target of 34 million is about 80% met.

Dai-ichi Life also notes reports that the government intends to set a target of lifting equities, investment trusts and bonds to 40% of household financial assets by fiscal 2040 — against 25.2% today. Treat that as reported intent rather than settled policy, but the direction of travel is not ambiguous.

Why this is hard to see from outside Japan

Three things get lost in translation, and all three are visible only in the Japanese tables.

The first is the deposit distinction. English summaries collapse everything into "deposits." The Japanese statistics separate liquidity deposits from time deposits, and the entire story of this quarter lives in the gap between them. A reader working from the English release would see a modest rise in total deposits and conclude nothing happened.

The second is that this release carried retroactive revisions. The end-2025 household total was previously reported around ¥2,351 trillion and now reads ¥2,394.1 trillion. Anyone comparing a fresh figure against an older write-up is comparing two different series.

The third is a genuine turn that went almost unremarked: on a seasonally adjusted basis, households moved into net financial deficit in the quarter, at −¥1.3 trillion against +¥4.9 trillion in October–December. Japanese households have been a reliable surplus sector for decades. Dai-ichi Life is cautious about it — seasonally adjusted figures for this series swing hard, and the four-quarter average still shows a surplus — but it is worth a marker.

What to watch

The next flow of funds release covers April to June and lands in September. Dai-ichi Life expects it to set a fresh record for household assets, on the strength of the equity rally through June — a valuation effect, not a flow.

Two other things sit underneath. The BOJ's ownership share of Japanese government bonds fell to 42.21% from 43.10%, a ninth consecutive quarterly decline; somebody has to hold the paper the central bank is no longer buying, and Japanese households are being actively courted as part of the answer. Watch whether the promised improvements to retail JGB terms actually appear.

And watch the ratio, not the headline. If the cash share keeps falling while investment trust inflows plateau, the money is moving inside the safe bucket, and the "savings to investment" narrative is a weaker signal than it looks.
