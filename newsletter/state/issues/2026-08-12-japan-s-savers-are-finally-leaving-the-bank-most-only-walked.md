# Japan's savers are finally leaving the bank. Most only walked next door.

*Ordinary deposits just had their biggest outflow on record — and the money is not going where you think.*

- 配信日: 2026-08-12
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**
2026年1-3月期の資金循環統計で、家計の普通預金からの純流出が過去最大になった。ただしその資金の多くは株式や投資信託ではなく、定期預金・国債・保険年金といった「安全資産の中での移動」に向かっている。海外メディアは現預金比率の低下をリスク選好の高まりとして報じがちだが、実態の相当部分は25年ぶりに金利が戻ったことによる資産内の裁定であり、性質が違う。

**使った数字と出典の対応**
- 2,385.7兆円 — 2026年3月末の家計金融資産残高 — 日銀 資金循環統計 2026年1-3月期速報（第一生命経済研究所レポート経由）
- 2,394.1兆円 — 前期（2025年12月末）残高。過去最高 — 同上
- +158.9兆円 — 前年同期差（ニッセイは159兆円と表記） — ニッセイ基礎研究所
- 47.2%（前期47.6%） — 家計金融資産に占める現預金比率 — 第一生命経済研究所
- 25.2%（前期24.6%） — 株式・投資信託・債券の比率 — 同上
- +5.8兆円 — 家計の資産側フロー4四半期移動平均。内訳は現預金+1.7、株式等・投信+1.5（うち投信+3.0）、債務証券+1.3、保険年金+1.3 — 同上
- ▲1.3兆円 — 家計の資金過不足（季調値）。前期+4.9兆円から資金不足に転じた — 同上
- 「流動性預金の純流出は過去最大」「投信の純流入は過去2番目」 — ニッセイ基礎研究所
- 42.21%（前期43.10%）、9四半期連続低下 — 日銀の国債保有比率 — 第一生命経済研究所
- 約71兆円（前年比+36%）、約2,826万口座 — NISA累計買付額と口座数、2025年12月末時点の速報値 — 金融庁（2026-02-18公表、日経報道経由）
- 669兆9702億円（約670兆円） — 2025年度の名目GDP — 内閣府（日経報道経由）。本文の「約3.5倍」はこれと2,385.7兆円から算出
- 2% — 長期貸出金利、25年ぶりの水準 — ニッセイ基礎研究所（26年6月の貸出・マネタリー統計解説）

**確信度が低い箇所**
4点ある。前回から変更なし。
1. 2025年12月末の残高について、ニッセイの前回レポートは2,351兆円としているが、今回の速報は遡及改定を含み、第一生命は前期を2,394.1兆円としている。本文は改定後を採用した。改定の詳細は日銀の原資料まで当たれていない。
2. NISAの数値は金融庁の公表を日経報道経由で取得した。金融庁の該当ページ自体は取得できず、内訳（つみたて枠と成長枠の別、商品別）は未確認のため本文でも触れていない。
3. 「2040年度までに4割へ」は第一生命レポートが「報じられている」と記載しているもので、確定した政府目標ではない。本文でも報道ベースであることを明示した。
4. 「約3.5倍」は2,385.7兆円÷669.97兆円＝約3.56倍の丸め。2つの統計は基準時点が異なる（残高とフロー）ため、厳密な比較ではなく規模感の目安。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**
個別銘柄・ファンド・通貨への言及なし。価格予想なし。「買うべき」に類する表現なし。将来見通しは第一生命の見解として発言者に帰属させた。政府目標は報道ベースであることを明示。今回は構成を箇条書き中心に組み替えたが、要約する過程で断定が強まっていないかを確認し、「reported intent rather than settled policy」「a valuation effect, not people putting money in」などの留保はすべて残してある。問題は認められない。

<p>Imagine leaving your salary in a bank account for twenty-five years and earning, in total, almost nothing. Not a bad year. Not a rough decade. Nothing, as a matter of policy, for an entire working life.</p>

<p>That was the deal for Japanese households, and they took it anyway. Last quarter, they walked away from it faster than they ever have.</p>

<h2>The short version</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Cash and deposits fell to <strong>47.2%</strong> of household financial assets. That share sat above 50% for decades.</li>
  <li>Ordinary deposits saw their <strong>largest net outflow on record</strong>.</li>
  <li>But deposits as a whole are <em>still growing</em>. Most of the money moved to time deposits and bonds &#8212; not to stocks.</li>
  <li>The reason is boring and enormous: after a generation at zero, safe assets in Japan pay interest again.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The headline number</h2>

<p><strong>Japanese households hold &#165;2,385.7 trillion &#8212; about three and a half times everything Japan produces in a year.</strong></p>

<p>The Bank of Japan's <a href="https://www.boj.or.jp/statistics/sj/index.htm">flow of funds accounts</a> for January to March:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Cash and deposits: <strong>47.2%</strong> of the total, down from 47.6%</li>
  <li>Equities, funds and bonds: <strong>25.2%</strong>, up from 24.6%</li>
  <li>Total assets grew <strong>&#165;158.9 trillion</strong> over twelve months</li>
</ul>

<p>So far, so tidy. Japan's cautious savers are moving into markets, the thirty-year story finally turns, and you have probably read a version of that sentence this summer.</p>

<h2>Where the money actually went</h2>

<p><strong>Out of the current account &#8212; and mostly into another safe asset down the hall.</strong></p>

<p>Smooth the flows over four quarters and households added &#165;5.8 trillion:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Destination</th><th>Four-quarter average flow</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Cash and deposits</td><td>+&#165;1.7tn</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Equities and investment trusts</td><td>+&#165;1.5tn (funds alone +&#165;3.0tn)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Debt securities</td><td>+&#165;1.3tn</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Insurance and pensions</td><td>+&#165;1.3tn</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Look at the first row. Deposits as a category are still growing. What is collapsing is one particular kind &#8212; the liquidity deposit, the ordinary account your salary lands in, paying next to nothing.</p>

<p>Same building. Different room.</p>

<p>Long-term bank lending rates crossed <strong>2% in June, a twenty-five-year high</strong>. A household moving from a current account to a time deposit has not developed an appetite for risk. It has noticed that interest exists again.</p>

<h2>The line nobody is quoting</h2>

<p><strong>Equities and funds together took in &#165;1.5 trillion. Funds alone took in &#165;3.0 trillion.</strong></p>

<p>Japanese households are buying investment trusts and net selling individual shares at the same time. The "retail is coming for Japanese equities" story is, at the household level, mostly a story about funds.</p>

<p>The fund channel is genuinely large: cumulative buying through NISA &#8212; Japan's tax-free investment account &#8212; reached roughly <strong>&#165;71 trillion</strong> across about <strong>28.3 million accounts</strong> by end-December, up 36% in a year. The &#165;56 trillion target set for 2027 was cleared in March 2025. Dai-ichi Life's economists also flag reports that the government wants equities, funds and bonds at 40% of household assets by fiscal 2040, against 25.2% now &#8212; reported intent rather than settled policy, but nobody is being coy about the direction.</p>

<h2>Why this is hard to see from outside Japan</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>English coverage says "deposits." The Japanese tables say two different things.</strong> They separate <em>liquidity</em> deposits from <em>time</em> deposits, and this quarter's entire story is the gap between them. From the English summary you would see total deposits tick up and conclude nothing happened.</li>
  <li><strong>This release quietly revised history.</strong> The end-2025 household total was previously reported around &#165;2,351 trillion and now reads &#165;2,394.1 trillion. Compare a fresh figure against a spring article and you are comparing two different series.</li>
  <li><strong>Households became net borrowers and almost nobody reported it.</strong> Seasonally adjusted, the household balance went to <strong>&#8722;&#165;1.3 trillion</strong> from +&#165;4.9 trillion. They have been a dependable surplus sector for decades. Dai-ichi Life is careful &#8212; this series swings hard once adjusted, and the four-quarter average is still in surplus &#8212; but it is worth a pin in the map.</li>
</ol>

<h2>What to watch</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>September:</strong> the April&#8211;June release. Dai-ichi Life expects a record for household assets on the equity rally &#8212; a valuation effect, not people putting money in.</li>
  <li><strong>Retail JGBs:</strong> the BOJ now owns <strong>42.21%</strong> of Japanese government bonds, down for a ninth straight quarter. Someone has to hold what the central bank stopped buying, and households are being courted for the job. Improvements to retail JGB terms have been trailed; watch whether they arrive.</li>
  <li><strong>The ratio, not the headline:</strong> if the cash share keeps sliding while fund inflows flatten, the money is just circulating inside the safe bucket &#8212; and "savings to investment" is a weaker signal than it looks.</li>
</ul>
