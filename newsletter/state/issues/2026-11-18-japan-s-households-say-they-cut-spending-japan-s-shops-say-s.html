Every English write-up of Japan's third-quarter GDP said the same thing, and it was a fair reading of the headline: the economy shrank because households stopped spending. I went into the Cabinet Office's own spreadsheets to see how much they had stopped, and found two answers.

Attached to the same release, in a reference table almost nobody opens, are two estimates of what households spent last quarter. One is built from what households report. The other from what shops and service firms report. Last quarter the first fell and the second rose, and the distance between them is the widest in the thirty-two years the series covers.

The short version

  • Real GDP fell at an annualised 0.1% in July to September, the first contraction in four quarters, and household consumption did the shrinking.
  • By households' own numbers, they spent 0.9% less in yen than a year earlier. By shops' numbers, 4.4% more.
  • The two estimates sit ¥3.1 trillion apart in one quarter — about one yen in twelve, and the biggest gap since the series starts in 1994.
  • All of it happened while real employee compensation rose 2.4% on the year. Whatever households did, they did it while getting better off.

What the headline says

The economy shrank by roughly a rounding error, and consumption is why.

From the first preliminary estimate of 16 November, seasonally adjusted:

  • Real GDP — −0.02% on the quarter · −0.1% annualised · first fall since Q3 2025
  • Household consumption — −0.3% · −1.3% annualised · second consecutive fall
  • Durable goods — −3.5% · −13.2% annualised
  • Services — +0.1% · +0.3% annualised
  • Real compensation of employees — +2.4% on the year

Here is the part that trips everyone up, including me. In yen, households spent more: nominal consumption rose 0.4% on the quarter, from ¥348.6 trillion to ¥350.0 trillion at an annual rate. The whole real decline is the price deflator. Households handed over more money and came home with less.

Two numbers for the same thing, in the same release

The Cabinet Office cannot tell you exactly what households spent, and it admits so in public every quarter.

Japan's national accounts build household consumption twice. The demand-side estimate comes from surveys of households, what people say they bought. The supply-side estimate comes from retail and services statistics, what businesses say they sold. The published GDP figure draws on both, and the two raw estimates go out side by side in a reference file, in nominal terms, so anyone can see how far apart they are.

  • Demand-side estimate — ¥37.4tn for the quarter · −0.9% on the year
  • Supply-side estimate — ¥40.5tn · +4.4% on the year
  • The gap — ¥3.1tn · 8.3% of the demand-side figure
  • Gap between the growth rates — 5.3 percentage points · widest since the series begins in Q1 1994
  • Previous widest — 4.1 points, in Q1 2009

Annualised, that disagreement runs at roughly ¥12 trillion a year against a nominal economy of ¥693 trillion. Call it 1.8% of GDP that nobody can locate.

I cannot tell you which one is right, and neither can the Cabinet Office — that is why it publishes both. What I can say is that the gap is at a record, one series fell while the other rose, and Monday's headline sits on top of that.

Part of the fall is the government paying the bill

Two of the deepest cuts in the household survey are policy, not thrift.

The Family Income and Expenditure Survey for September, out on 10 November, is the gloomiest of the lot: ¥289,536 per household, down 6.8% in real terms, a tenth consecutive monthly fall, every category negative, and 4.5% down on a three-month average. Then look at which categories led it.

  • Education — −23.5% in real terms on the year
  • Utilities — −10.5% · seventh straight fall
  • Transport and communication — −9.1% · the largest single drag
  • Recreation — −8.7%
  • Food — −2.5% · fourth straight fall

On 1 April 2026 Japan abolished the income cap on high-school tuition support and raised the private-school ceiling to ¥457,200 a year, from ¥396,000. The money goes to the school, not the parent, so it never shows up as household income and it does show up as a collapse in household education spending. In this survey, a family whose tuition bill was just cancelled looks exactly like a family that gave up on school.

Utilities work the same way. The government discounted electricity for July, August and September usage at ¥3.5, ¥4.5 and ¥3.5 per kilowatt-hour, straight onto the bill, no application. The 2025 summer discount was smaller, so part of this year's fall is the subsidy growing rather than households using less. Only part: water and sewerage fell too, and no subsidy touched those.

So where did the money go? Toward the longer-dated end of the bank: in the Bank of Japan's October money stock, time and savings deposits were up 4.4% while demand deposits shrank 0.2% and cash in circulation shrank 1.5%. That series counts companies too, so read it as a direction, not a household number.

72.7% — the share of take-home pay Japanese worker households spent in September, against 82.1% a year earlier.

Why this is hard to see from outside Japan

  1. The two parallel estimates live in a Japanese-only reference CSV attached to the release. No English version, and no press line pointing at it.
  2. The tuition change took effect on 1 April 2026. It lands as a fall in household spending rather than a rise in household income, and no English coverage of the GDP number mentioned it.
  3. The energy discount is applied by the utility before the bill is issued. Households never see a transfer, and the statistics never record one.
  4. Japan runs two official measures of household spending. For September the household survey said −6.8% on the year and the national accounts said −0.3%. English coverage quotes whichever it found first.
  5. The Monthly Labour Survey gives two real-wage figures for September, deflated two ways: +2.2% and +2.3%. And the 4.6% rise in total cash earnings was flattered by special payments, up 32.6%; scheduled pay, the clean number, rose 3.7%.

What to watch

  • Friday 20 November, 8:30am Tokyo — October national CPI. September ran at +2.1%. This is the deflator that decides whether real wages stay positive for a tenth month.
  • The second estimate of third-quarter GDP, due in December — and whether the revision moves toward the supply-side figure or the demand-side one.
  • October's household survey — the energy discount covered usage through September, so October is the first month without it. If utilities stop falling, we learn how much of the slump was the subsidy.

What I keep turning over is this. The Bank of Japan reads this consumption signal when it sets rates, and the signal is assembled from two sources ¥3.1 trillion apart. If you remember one thing, make it that Monday's headline was a blend of a number that fell and a number that rose. If you know a sharper way to tell which of the two is lying, hit reply.
