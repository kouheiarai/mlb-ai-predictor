# Japan's cash pile fell below 45% of household assets. Nobody moved it.

*Deposits grew 0.5% over the year. Equities grew 47%. The share fell because the denominator did all the work.*

- 配信日: 2026-10-07
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**

9月17日公表の資金循環統計（2026年第2四半期）で、家計の現金・預金比率が**44.9％**まで下がった。「ついに貯蓄から投資へ」と報じられる数字だが、**中身を見ると家計はほとんど何も動かしていない。** 現金・預金は前年比＋0.5％で、残高はむしろ増えている（1,126兆円→1,132兆円）。比率が下がったのは分母——株式＋47.0％、投資信託＋36.8％——が膨らんだため。つまり価格の上昇であって資産配分の変更ではない。1号目で「家計は個別株を売り越していた」と書いたことと合わせると、買って増えたのではないという読みが補強される。

**あわせて、1号目で出した47.2％という数字の訂正を載せる。**

**使った数字と出典の対応**

- 2,519兆円（前年比＋11.0％） — 家計の金融資産残高（2026年6月末） — 日本銀行 資金循環統計 2026年第2四半期速報、2026年9月17日公表
- 1,132兆円（44.9％、前年比＋0.5％） — うち現金・預金 — 同上
- 486兆円（19.3％、前年比＋47.0％） — うち株式等 — 同上
- 193兆円（7.7％、前年比＋36.8％） — うち投資信託 — 同上
- 586兆円（23.3％、前年比＋3.2％） — うち保険・年金等 — 同上
- 38兆円（1.5％、前年比＋15.5％） — うち債務証券 — 同上
- 416兆円／397兆円 — 家計の負債合計／うち借入（2026年6月末） — 同上
- 2,406兆円 — 2026年3月末の家計金融資産（Q2公表時点の改定値） — 同上
- 2,386兆円／1,126兆円／47.2％ — 同（Q1公表時点の当初値） — 日本銀行 2026年第1四半期速報、2026年6月25日公表

- 2,197兆円 — 2024年9月末の家計金融資産 — 同上
- 約15％ — 2,197兆円→2,519兆円の2年間の増加（本稿での計算） — 上記より算出

**確信度が低い箇所**

- **取引フロー（実際にいくら買ったか）は使えていない。** Q2資料のフローは図表で、本稿の抽出では数値を取り出せなかった。したがって「価格上昇が主因」という結論は、残高の伸び率（預金＋0.5％に対し株式＋47.0％）からの推論であって、フローとバリュエーションを分解した数字ではない。本文でもその旨を明示している。**断定を避け、「buying alone cannot plausibly explain it」という書き方にしてある。**
- 株式＋47.0％の内訳（どの程度が株価上昇か、どの程度が純購入か）は確認していない。
- 2026年3月末の現金・預金比率「約46.8％」は、改定後の総額2,406兆円に、Q1公表時点の預金残高1,126兆円を当てた本稿の計算。**預金残高そのものも改定されている可能性があるが、Q2資料には3月末の預金残高が明示されていないため確認できていない。** 本文でも「implied」と書いて推計であることを示した。
- 比率の変化（47.2％→44.9％）は、当初値と改定後の値をまたいだ比較になっている。改定後ベースで揃えると46.8％→44.9％。本文では両方書いた。
- 1号目の「家計は投資信託を買い、個別株を売り越した」はQ1のフローデータに基づく記述。Q2でも同じ傾向が続いたかは確認していない。本文では「Q1で観測された」と期間を限定している。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**

- 個別銘柄・商品の推奨なし。株式が47％増えたという事実は書いているが、買うべきとは書いていない。
- 株価の見通しなし。今後も上がるか下がるかには触れていない。むしろ「価格で増えた資産は価格で減りうる」という対称性を1行入れた。
- 「貯蓄から投資へ」という政策的な評価をしていない。数字がその物語を支持しないと書いただけ。
- **訂正を載せている。** 自分が8月に出した数字が改定された事実を、読者から見える形で書いた。house style の「数字は日付と出典つき、検証できなければそう書く」に沿った処理。

<h2>The numerator did not move</h2>

<p>Deposits grew 0.5% over the year. Not fell &mdash; grew, from about &yen;1,126 trillion to &yen;1,132 trillion. Japanese households did not take money out of the bank. They put slightly more in.</p>

<p>Equity holdings grew 47.0%. Investment trusts grew 36.8%.</p>

<p>That is the entire explanation for the falling cash share. The denominator inflated and the numerator sat still, so the ratio fell. Arithmetic, not behaviour.</p>

<p>Could households have bought their way to a 47% increase? In principle. In practice, that would mean purchasing something like &yen;155 trillion of shares in twelve months &mdash; and in the first quarter of this year, as this newsletter reported in August, households were <em>net sellers</em> of individual equities even while buying funds. People who are net sellers of a thing do not increase their holdings of it by half through buying.</p>

<p>I want to be careful here, because I could not extract the transaction flows from this release &mdash; they are published as charts rather than tables. So this is an inference from growth rates, not a decomposition. But no plausible split rescues the rotation story. Prices did this.</p>

<h2>A correction</h2>

<p>In August this newsletter reported the cash-and-deposit share at 47.2%, from the first-quarter release.</p>

<p>That release put household assets at &yen;2,386 trillion as of end-March. <strong>The second-quarter release revises that figure to &yen;2,406 trillion</strong> &mdash; twenty trillion yen higher. Against the revised total, the same deposit balance implies a share of roughly 46.8%, not 47.2%.</p>

<p>It is a small difference and it does not change anything that issue argued. I am flagging it because quarterly statistics get revised, almost nobody who quotes them goes back to check, and a newsletter that asks you to trust its numbers should tell you when one of them moves.</p>

<p>Measured consistently on the revised basis, the share went from about 46.8% in March to 44.9% in June. Still a fall. Still almost entirely valuation.</p>

<h2>The line nobody reads</h2>

<p>While everyone argues about cash versus stocks, look at the second row of that table.</p>

<p>Insurance and pensions: <strong>&yen;586 trillion</strong>. That is more than Japanese households hold in equities, and it is 23.3% of everything they own &mdash; a quarter of the pile, sitting in a category that gets discussed almost nowhere.</p>

<p>It grew 3.2% over the year. Not 47%, not 0.5%: 3.2%. It behaves like neither cash nor equity, because much of it is a contractual claim whose value does not swing with the market and does not sit in a bank either.</p>

<p>The conventional frame for Japan is a binary &mdash; money is either dead in deposits or alive in the stock market. The actual balance sheet has a third block nearly as large as the first, and it has been quietly compounding at low single digits while the argument goes on without it.</p>

<h2>Why the number keeps moving</h2>

<p>On the two-year view, household assets went from &yen;2,197 trillion at the end of September 2024 to &yen;2,519 trillion now &mdash; up about 15%. Japanese households got meaningfully richer on paper in two years, in a country whose savers are routinely described as stuck.</p>

<p>Paper is the operative word, and it cuts both ways: a balance sheet that grew mostly through valuation is a balance sheet that can shrink the same way, without anybody doing anything.</p>

<h2>What a real rotation would look like</h2>

<p>It would start with the deposit line going <em>down</em> in absolute yen, not up 0.5%. Households would be withdrawing, not merely failing to add.</p>

<p>That has not happened. What has happened, over the issues this newsletter has covered since August, is narrower and more consistent: money leaving the ordinary current account for time deposits and government bonds, which is a response to interest rates returning rather than an appetite for risk; and a tax-free wrapper being widened to admit bond funds, which is the government responding to the same preference.</p>

<p>Meanwhile the equity and fund holdings that Japanese households already owned went up a great deal in price, and the ratio everyone quotes moved as a result.</p>

<p>There is a symmetry worth holding onto. A share that fell because asset prices rose will rise again if asset prices fall, without a single household deciding anything. The 44.9% is a fact about markets at least as much as it is a fact about savers.</p>

<p>One more line from the same release, since this newsletter spent last month on the other side of the ledger: household liabilities now stand at &yen;416 trillion, of which &yen;397 trillion is borrowings. Both up slightly on the quarter. That side is not a valuation effect. It is money owed, at a rate the Bank of Japan has now raised twice this year.</p>
