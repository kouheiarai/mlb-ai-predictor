On 3 August, the ordinary deposit account at Japan's three megabanks started paying 0.4% a year. For Mitsubishi UFJ and Sumitomo Mitsui, that is the highest rate in about 34 years — which puts the last comparable rate in the early 1990s, before a good share of the country's working population was born.

Eighteen days later, the Statistics Bureau published July's consumer price index. All items, up 1.9% on the year.

Those two numbers belong side by side, and almost nobody puts them there.

Put ¥1,000,000 in a megabank ordinary account and leave it there for a year. You earn ¥4,000 before tax. At 1.9% inflation, the same ¥1,000,000 buys about ¥19,000 less than it did. You are down roughly ¥15,000, and you were paid to be there.

This is a crude calculation and I want to be honest about that. It ignores tax on the interest, which is real and makes the ¥4,000 smaller. It uses the national CPI rather than whatever you personally buy. But the direction does not depend on the refinements. Cash in an ordinary Japanese bank account is still losing, after the biggest increase in the rate it pays in three decades.

Now multiply it

The Bank of Japan's own flow of funds chartbook puts household financial assets at ¥2,386 trillion at the end of March, of which currency and deposits account for ¥1,126 trillion — 47.2% of everything Japanese households own.

Run a 1.5% real drag across a pool that size and you are talking about something on the order of ¥17 trillion of purchasing power a year.

Treat that as scale, not precision. The pool is not uniform: physical cash earns nothing at all and does worse than the ordinary account, time deposits do better, and the mix shifts every quarter. But there is no assumption you can make about the mix that turns the sign positive. The largest concentration of household savings in the developed world is being slowly charged rent.

How Japan got here

On 16 June the Bank of Japan moved the uncollateralised overnight call rate to around 1.0%. The megabanks announced their deposit increase the same day, to take effect seven weeks later.

Worth noting in the statement: the vote was seven to one. Not unanimous. The Bank has been moving, but not without argument inside the room.

The pass-through is the part to watch. The policy rate is at 1.0%. The ordinary deposit pays 0.4%. Banks are not obliged to hand the full move to depositors and they have not, which is ordinary banking and also the reason the saver's arithmetic still comes out negative. A policy rate that clears inflation does not mean a deposit rate that clears inflation.

Why the money moved, in one line

Two weeks ago this newsletter looked at the flow of funds and found households pulling record sums out of ordinary deposits, mostly into time deposits, government bonds and insurance rather than equities. At the time the read was that this was a rate story, not a risk-appetite story.

Now put the table above next to that behaviour. Households sitting in the ordinary account were watching their money shrink by about 1.5% a year in real terms. Moving down the hall to a time deposit does not make that positive — but it narrows the loss, without asking anyone to develop a view on equities.

Seen from here, that looks less like caution and more like arithmetic.

Two things underneath

First, the dispersion. The three megabanks moved together to the same number, which is what they do. Elsewhere in the market the spread has widened considerably — several online banks are paying multiples of the megabank rate on ordinary balances. For most of the last twenty years, comparing Japanese deposit rates was a waste of an afternoon, because every option rounded to zero. It is no longer a waste of an afternoon. Whether households treat it that way is a different question, and the flow of funds suggests many of them have not started.

Second, the index itself. July's release is the first on a 2025 base year, rebased from 2020. The basket and the weights have been refreshed to reflect what households now buy. Rebasing does not change what is happening to prices, but it does mean that comparisons with the old series should be handled carefully, and that anyone quoting an unusually neat year-on-year figure for Japan this month may be comparing two slightly different rulers.

What this actually means

The story English-language coverage has settled on is that Japan has exited deflation and savers are being rewarded again. Half of that is right. Rates are genuinely back, the numbers are the highest in three decades, and that is a real change in the country's financial physics.

But the reward is not yet a reward. On the largest pool of household cash in the developed world, the return net of inflation is still negative — less negative than it was, which matters, and negative all the same.

Japan spent thirty years teaching its households that where you keep money does not matter, because nothing paid anything. That lesson has now expired. It will take longer than eighteen days for people to notice.
