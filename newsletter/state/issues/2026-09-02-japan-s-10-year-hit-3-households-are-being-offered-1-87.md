# Japan's 10-year hit 3%. Households are being offered 1.87%.

*The retail government bond pays two-thirds of the market rate — a haircut designed for a world where rates were zero.*

- 配信日: 2026-09-02
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**

9月1日に10年国債利回りが3％に乗り、1996年以来の水準になった。しかし**家計が3％を買えるわけではない。** 個人向け国債の適用利率は基準金利に0.66を掛ける（変動10年）などの式で決まっており、9月発行分は変動10年1.87％、固定5年2.06％、固定3年1.71％。7月のCPI（総合＋1.9％）と比べると、**固定5年だけがかろうじて実質プラス（＋0.16％）**で、他は実質マイナス。0.66という掛け目は金利ゼロの時代に作られたもので、市場金利が3％になったいま、家計は1ポイント超を差し出している。3号目で見た普通預金0.4％と並べると、銀行と国のあいだに約1.7ポイントの差がある。

**確信度が低い箇所**

- **個人向け国債の利率が一次資料で取れていない。** 財務省の該当ページを3つ試したがいずれも404で、数字は専門メディア（money-pro.net）と検索結果の記述に依存している。複数の情報源で1.87／2.06／1.71と一致しているため採用したが、**公開前に財務省サイトで確認することを強く勧める。** ここが間違っていると記事の骨格が崩れる。
- 回号（第197回・第185回・第195回）も同じ出所。本文では回号に触れていない。
- 0.66という掛け目が「いつ・どういう趣旨で決まったか」は確認していない。本文では「ゼロ金利の時代に作られた設計」と書いているが、これは制度の効果についての解釈であって、財務省がそう説明しているわけではない。断定を避けた書き方にしてある。
- 実質利回りは各商品の表面利率から総合CPIを引いた単純計算で、利子課税を含まない。本文で「税引前」と明示している。課税後は3商品とも実質マイナスになる可能性が高いが、税率を一次資料で確認していないため本文では計算していない。
- 「1996年以来」はBloombergとEuronewsの見出しに基づく。両社とも同日同内容なので信頼できるが、日本の一次データ（財務省の金利公表）では確認していない。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**

- **個別商品の推奨をしていないか特に注意した号。** 個人向け国債という具体的な商品を扱っているため、「買うべき」「銀行から移すべき」に類する表現がないか通しで確認した。書いているのは利率と物価の差という事実と、利率がどう決まるかという仕組みだけ。
- 固定5年だけが実質プラスであることを書いているが、「だから固定5年を買え」とは書いていない。むしろ差がごくわずか（0.16ポイント）で、税引後には消えうることを明示している。
- 金利予想なし。今後利回りがどうなるかは書いていない。木内氏の「3％は通過点か」という論考は出典に挙げているが、本文でその見通しを引用してはいない。
- 「1996年以来」等の事実はすべて報道に帰属させている。

<p>On 1 September the yield on Japan's 10-year government bond <a href="https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/09/01/japans-10-year-yield-hits-3-a-first-since-1996-as-rate-hikes-dominate-the-g20">touched 3%</a>, up six basis points on the day and the first time it has been there since 1996. A year ago it was around half that.</p>

<p>Every wire ran it. All of them framed it the same way: a bond market story, about rate-hike expectations and the fiscal outlook. Which it is.</p>

<p>Nobody asked the question a Japanese household would ask, which is whether any of that 3% is available to them.</p>

<p>It is not. Households do not buy the 10-year at auction. What they can buy is the retail government bond, and its rate is set by a formula that hands them a fraction of the market rate.</p>

<h2>The 0.66</h2>

<p>Look at the fixed five-year line. <strong>2.06% against 1.9% inflation.</strong> After a generation in which no safe yen asset cleared inflation, one of them now does &mdash; by sixteen basis points, before tax on the interest. Call that a tie rather than a win, and it is still the first tie in thirty years.</p>

<p>Then look at the multiplier on the line below it.</p>

<h2>A haircut designed for zero</h2>

<p>The variable ten-year product pays the base rate times 0.66. The saver gives up a third of the rate in exchange for a bond whose coupon resets with the market rather than locking in.</p>

<p>For most of the time this product has existed, that trade was invisible. When the base rate was 0.2%, two-thirds of it was 0.13%, and the difference was a rounding error on a number nobody was reading anyway.</p>

<p>At 3%, two-thirds is 1.98%. The haircut is now worth <strong>a full percentage point a year</strong> &mdash; on the product the government markets to households as the flexible, safe, inflation-tracking choice.</p>

<p>The formula did not change. The world it was calibrated for did.</p>

<h2>What the 0.66 actually buys</h2>

<p>In fairness to the product, the multiplier is not simply a fee. The variable ten-year resets its coupon against the prevailing base rate, so a household buying at 1.87% this month is not fixed there &mdash; if rates keep climbing, the coupon follows. The fixed five-year has the opposite shape: better today at 2.06%, and then frozen for five years regardless of what inflation does next.</p>

<p>That is a genuine trade, and for the first time in decades it is a trade worth thinking about. Two years ago both products rounded to nothing and the choice between them was academic. The interesting thing is not that one of them is better. It is that the question has a live answer at all.</p>

<p>What the multiplier does is decide how much of the market's move reaches the person taking the other side of it. At 0.2% that share was a technicality. At 3% it is the difference between clearing inflation comfortably and not clearing it.</p>

<h2>The September buyer is trading on August's information</h2>

<p>One more wrinkle, and it is the kind of thing that only shows up if you read the schedule rather than the headline.</p>

<p>The rates in that table were set for a subscription window that ran from 6 to 31 August, for bonds issued on 15 September. The 10-year touched 3% on 1 September &mdash; after the window closed, before the bonds exist.</p>

<p>So a household buying this month's issue is locked to the rate environment of three weeks ago. Not a scandal, just how a monthly issuance calendar works. But it means the gap between the market and the household is wider right now than the table makes it look, and it will keep reopening every time the market moves inside a subscription window.</p>

<h2>What this is really measuring</h2>

<p>Set the retail bond next to the bank. The fixed five-year pays 2.06%. The megabank ordinary account, at its highest in about 34 years, pays 0.40%. That is a gap of roughly <strong>1.7 percentage points</strong> between the two safest places a Japanese household can put money.</p>

<p>One caution on the plus sign in that table. The +0.16% on the fixed five-year is calculated before tax on the interest, and interest in Japan is taxed. Run the tax through and the margin is thin enough that it may not survive. So the honest statement is not that the retail bond now beats inflation &mdash; it is that it has stopped clearly losing to it, which after thirty years is still a different world.</p>

<p>For thirty years that gap was close enough to nothing that the choice did not matter, which is exactly why so few households ever made it. Japanese savers are often described as irrationally attached to cash. It is worth considering the simpler explanation: for most of their adult lives, the alternative paid nothing either, and a habit formed under those conditions is not irrational, just out of date.</p>

<p>It has taken a 30-year high in the bond market to make the arithmetic legible again. And the answer it gives is oddly modest: not "buy bonds", but "the safest thing available has finally stopped losing, by a margin thin enough that tax could take it back".</p>

<p>That is not a stampede. It is a country slowly rediscovering that where you keep money is a question with an answer.</p>
