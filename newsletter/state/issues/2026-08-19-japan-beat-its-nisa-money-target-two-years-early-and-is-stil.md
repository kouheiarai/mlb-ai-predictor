# Japan beat its NISA money target two years early — and is still 5.8m people short

*The fix it chose says more than the miss does: from January, the tax-free wrapper opens to bond funds and to children.*

- 配信日: 2026-08-19
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**

政府はNISAに2つの目標を置いた。買付額（2027年末までに56兆円）は2年前倒しで達成し、口座数（同3,400万口座）は579万口座届いていない。つまり「すでに始めた人がもっと入れている」だけで、新しい人には届いていない。2027年1月からの制度変更（こどもNISA、およびつみたて投資枠の対象商品拡充）は、この「深さは足りたが幅が足りない」という結果への対応として読める。特に後者——株式50％超という要件を株式＋公社債50％超に変える——は英語圏でまったく報じられていないが、1号目で見た「家計は安全な利回りに動いている」という事実と正面から噛み合う。

## 本文（この HTML を beehiiv に貼る）

```html
<p>Japan set two numbers for its tax-free investment accounts to hit by the end of 2027. One of them fell two years early. The other depends on a trend that is slowing down.</p>

<p>The Financial Services Agency published its <a href="https://www.fsa.go.jp/policy/nisa/20260703.html">tally for the end of December 2025</a> in July. NISA accounts: 28.21 million, against a government target of 34 million. Cumulative purchases through those accounts: &yen;71 trillion, against a target of &yen;56 trillion.</p>

<table>
  <tr><th></th><th>End-2023</th><th>End-2024</th><th>End-2025</th><th>End-2027 target</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Accounts</td><td>21.25m</td><td>25.59m</td><td>28.21m</td><td>34m</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Cumulative purchases</td><td>&yen;35tn</td><td>&yen;53tn</td><td>&yen;71tn</td><td>&yen;56tn</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Read those two rows together, because separately they tell opposite stories. Over two years, accounts grew 33%. Purchases grew 103%. Divide one by the other and average cumulative purchases per account went from about &yen;1.65m to about &yen;2.52m &mdash; that is money put in since the account opened, not what the account is worth today, and the distinction matters.</p>

<p>So the programme is not failing. It is succeeding at one thing and not the other. The people who signed up are putting in far more than anyone budgeted for. The 5.79 million people still needed to reach the account target have watched two years of headlines about NISA and remained exactly where they were.</p>

<p>And the recruiting is getting harder, which is the part worth watching. 2024 added 4.34 million accounts. 2025 added 2.62 million. Hold that second pace for two more years and Japan lands at roughly 33.5 million by the end of 2027 &mdash; close enough to look like a rounding error in a press release, short enough to be a miss. Let the deceleration continue at anything like its current rate and it is not close at all.</p>

<p>Which reframes the policy question. It is not "how do we get people to invest more", because they are. It is "who is left, and why have they not come".</p>

<h2>What the government did about it</h2>

<p>The FY2026 tax reform bill <a href="https://www.yamada-partners.jp/tax-topics/r080406">passed the upper house on 31 March</a>. Two changes to NISA came with it. One got the coverage. The other is the interesting one.</p>

<h3>The one that got covered</h3>

<p>From January 2027, the tsumitate frame &mdash; the conservative half of NISA, restricted to funds the FSA has approved for long-term accumulation &mdash; drops its age floor. Children aged 0 to 17 get their own allowance: <a href="https://www.fsa.go.jp/access/r7/270/270_03.pdf">&yen;600,000 a year, capped at &yen;6m</a>. Adults keep &yen;1.2m a year and an &yen;18m lifetime cap.</p>

<p>Japan tried this before. Junior NISA ran from 2016 and stopped accepting new accounts at the end of 2023, and the FSA's own write-up is unusually blunt about why: uptake was poor. Money was locked in until 18. The tax-free window ran five years, after which you had to file paperwork to keep the shelter. Parents looked at a product they could not touch if a job went wrong, and passed.</p>

<p>The new one is built differently on all three counts. Withdrawals become possible from age 12, provided the money goes to the child, the child signs to say so, and the guardian files with the institution. The tax-free period is indefinite. At 18 the account rolls into the adult tsumitate frame automatically, with no forms.</p>

<h3>The one that did not</h3>

<p>Buried further down the same document is a change to what the tsumitate frame is allowed to hold.</p>

<p>Funds that do not track a designated index &mdash; including actively managed ones &mdash; have needed equities to make up more than 50% of the portfolio to qualify. That threshold is being rewritten: equities <em>and bonds</em> combined above 50%. Two indices, the Yomiuri 333 and JPX Prime 150, join the designated list at the same time.</p>

<p>In practice, the old wording did most of its work by exclusion. A balanced fund holding 40% equities and 50% bonds is a perfectly ordinary long-term product, and it failed the test on the equity line alone. So the shelf inside the tsumitate frame skewed toward equity funds &mdash; not because the FSA had ruled against bonds, but because of how the threshold was drawn.</p>

<p>The FSA is direct about the intent. It expects the change to widen the range of bond-centred and balanced funds available in the frame, and describes the audience it has in mind as people who are cautious about taking risk and have not managed to start investing.</p>

<p>That is a regulator saying, in a policy explainer, that the tax-free wrapper it built has been the wrong shape for the people it most wants to reach.</p>

<h2>Why this connects to the deposits story</h2>

<p>Last week this newsletter looked at where household money actually went when it left the ordinary bank account, and the answer was not the stock market. It was time deposits, government bonds and insurance. Households were not developing an appetite for risk. They were responding to the return of interest rates.</p>

<p>Now look at the rule change again. Until January, a saver who wanted to hold bonds inside a tax-free account had a narrow menu, because the frame was written on the assumption that long-term accumulation means equities. From January, the wrapper accommodates what those households demonstrated they actually wanted.</p>

<p>Whether that converts into accounts is a separate question, and an open one. A tax shelter is not a reason to invest; it is a discount on a decision you were already close to making. The 5.79 million people Japan is short of its target have not told anyone why they are staying out, and it is possible that product range was never the binding constraint. Nobody has that answer yet, including the FSA.</p>

<p>What can be said is narrower, and worth holding onto. Japan spent a decade building a tax-free account for people who wanted to buy equities, hit its money target early because those people turned out to be enthusiastic, and has now quietly widened the door for everyone else.</p>

<p>The second group is much larger. It always was.</p>
```
