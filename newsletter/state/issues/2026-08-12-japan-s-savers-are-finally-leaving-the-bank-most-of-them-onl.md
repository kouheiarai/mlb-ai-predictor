# Japan's savers are finally leaving the bank. Most of them only walked next door.

*Ordinary deposits just saw their biggest outflow on record — and the money is not going where you think.*

- 配信日: 2026-08-12
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**
2026年1-3月期の資金循環統計で、家計の普通預金からの純流出が過去最大になった。ただしその資金の多くは株式や投資信託ではなく、定期預金・国債・保険年金といった「安全資産の中での移動」に向かっている。海外メディアは現預金比率の低下をリスク選好の高まりとして報じがちだが、実態の相当部分は25年ぶりに金利が戻ったことによる資産内の裁定であり、性質が違う。

**使った数字と出典の対応**
- 2,385.7兆円 — 2026年3月末の家計金融資産残高 — 日銀 資金循環統計 2026年1-3月期速報（第一生命経済研究所レポート経由）
- 2,394.1兆円 — 前期（2025年12月末）残高。過去最高 — 同上
- +158.9兆円 — 前年同期差（ニッセイは159兆円と表記） — ニッセイ基礎研究所
- 47.2%（前期47.6%） — 家計金融資産に占める現預金比率 — 第一生命経済研究所
- 25.2%（前期24.6%） — 株式・投資信託・債券の比率 — 同上
- +5.8兆円 — 家計の資産側フロー4四半期移動平均。内訳は現預金+1.7、株式等・投信+1.5（うち投信+3.0）、債務証券+1.3、保険年金+1.3 — 同上
- ▲1.3兆円 — 家計の資金過不足（季調値）。前期+4.9兆円から資金不足に転じた — 同上
- 「流動性預金の純流出は過去最大」「投信の純流入は過去2番目」 — ニッセイ基礎研究所
- 42.21%（前期43.10%）、9四半期連続低下 — 日銀の国債保有比率 — 第一生命経済研究所
- 約71兆円（前年比+36%）、約2,826万口座（+10%） — NISA累計買付額と口座数、2025年12月末時点の速報値 — 金融庁（2026-02-18公表、日経報道経由）
- 669兆9702億円（約670兆円） — 2025年度の名目GDP、5年連続で過去最高 — 内閣府（日経報道経由）。本文の「約3.5倍」はこれと家計金融資産2,385.7兆円から算出
- 2% — 長期貸出金利、25年ぶりの水準 — ニッセイ基礎研究所（26年6月の貸出・マネタリー統計解説）

**確信度が低い箇所**
4点ある。
1. 2025年12月末の残高について、ニッセイの前回レポートは2,351兆円としているが、今回の速報は遡及改定を含み、第一生命は前期を2,394.1兆円としている。本文は改定後を採用した。改定の詳細は日銀の原資料まで当たれていない。
2. NISAの数値は金融庁の公表を日経報道経由で取得した。金融庁の該当ページ自体は取得できず、内訳（つみたて枠と成長枠の別、商品別）は未確認のため本文でも触れていない。
3. 「2040年度までに株式・投資信託・債券を4割へ」は第一生命レポートが「報じられている」と記載しているもので、確定した政府目標ではない。本文でも報道ベースであることを明示した。
4. 本文の「約3.5倍」は2,385.7兆円÷669.97兆円＝約3.56倍を丸めたもの。2つの統計は基準時点が異なる（前者は2026年3月末残高、後者は2025年度フロー）ため、厳密な比較ではなく規模感の目安である。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**
個別銘柄・ファンド・通貨への言及なし。価格予想なし。「買うべき」に類する表現なし。将来見通しは第一生命の見解（次回統計で過去最高更新）として発言者に帰属させた。政府目標は報道ベースであることを明示。文体を平易にした際に断定が強まっていないかを確認したが、事実と見通しの区別は保たれている。問題は認められない。

<p>Imagine leaving your salary in a bank account for twenty-five years and earning, in total, almost nothing. Not a bad year. Not a rough decade. Nothing, as a matter of policy, for an entire working life.</p>

<p>That was the deal for Japanese households, and they took it anyway. Roughly half of the largest private pool of savings on earth just sat in ordinary bank accounts, going quietly nowhere.</p>

<p>That deal is now ending, and last quarter it ended faster than it ever has.</p>

<h2>The number that got everyone's attention</h2>

<p>The Bank of Japan's <a href="https://www.boj.or.jp/statistics/sj/index.htm">flow of funds accounts</a> for January to March showed the biggest net outflow from ordinary deposits ever recorded. Cash and deposits fell to <strong>47.2%</strong> of household financial assets, from 47.6% three months earlier. That share sat above 50% for decades and only broke below it last year.</p>

<p>Households are sitting on ¥2,385.7 trillion in total. If that number is hard to picture, try this: it is about three and a half times everything Japan produces in a year. And it grew by ¥158.9 trillion over twelve months.</p>

<p>So far, so tidy. Japan's famously cautious savers are moving into markets, the thirty-year story finally turns, and you have probably read a version of that sentence this summer.</p>

<p>Here is the wrinkle.</p>

<h2>Where the money actually went</h2>

<p>Smooth the flows over four quarters and households added ¥5.8 trillion. Look at where it landed:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Destination</th><th>Four-quarter average flow</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Cash and deposits</td><td>+¥1.7tn</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Equities and investment trusts</td><td>+¥1.5tn (investment trusts alone +¥3.0tn)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Debt securities</td><td>+¥1.3tn</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Insurance and pensions</td><td>+¥1.3tn</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Look at the first row again. <strong>Deposits, as a category, are still growing.</strong></p>

<p>What is collapsing is one particular kind — the liquidity deposit, the ordinary account your salary lands in, paying next to nothing. The money walking out of it is mostly walking to the time deposit down the hall, or into a government bond, or into an insurance product. Same building. Different room.</p>

<p>Because after a generation in which every safe asset in Japan paid zero, several of them suddenly pay something. Long-term bank lending rates crossed 2% in June for the first time in twenty-five years. A household moving from a current account into a time deposit has not developed an appetite for risk. It has noticed that interest exists again.</p>

<p>One more detail worth pausing on. Equities and investment trusts together took in ¥1.5 trillion, but investment trusts <em>alone</em> took in ¥3.0 trillion. Japanese households are buying funds and selling individual shares at the same time.</p>

<h2>The investment story is real too</h2>

<p>None of this makes the other half small. Cumulative buying through NISA — Japan's tax-free investment account, and the government's main lever for prying money out of banks — hit roughly ¥71 trillion by the end of December, up 36% in a year, spread across about 28.3 million accounts. The ¥56 trillion target set for 2027 was cleared back in March 2025.</p>

<p>Dai-ichi Life's economists also flag reports that the government wants equities, funds and bonds to reach 40% of household assets by fiscal 2040. They are at 25.2% today. Treat that as reported intent rather than settled policy — but nobody is being coy about the direction.</p>

<h2>Why this is hard to see from outside Japan</h2>

<p>Three things, and all of them live in the Japanese tables.</p>

<p>The first is that deposit distinction. English coverage collapses everything into "deposits." The Japanese statistics keep <em>liquidity</em> deposits and <em>time</em> deposits apart, and the whole story of this quarter is the gap between them. Working from the English summary, you would see total deposits tick up and conclude nothing had happened.</p>

<p>The second is that this release quietly revised history. The end-2025 household total was previously reported around ¥2,351 trillion and now reads ¥2,394.1 trillion. If you compare a fresh figure against an article written in the spring, you are comparing two different series and will get a nonsense answer.</p>

<p>The third barely got reported at all: on a seasonally adjusted basis, <strong>households became net borrowers</strong> last quarter, at −¥1.3 trillion against +¥4.9 trillion in October–December. Japanese households have been a dependable surplus sector for decades. Dai-ichi Life is careful about it — this series swings hard once you seasonally adjust it, and the four-quarter average is still comfortably in surplus — but it is worth a pin in the map.</p>

<h2>What to watch</h2>

<p>The next release covers April to June and lands in September. Dai-ichi Life expects a fresh record for household assets on the back of the equity rally — worth remembering that is a valuation effect, not people putting money in.</p>

<p>Underneath sits a quieter problem. The BOJ now owns 42.21% of Japanese government bonds, down from 43.10% and falling for the ninth quarter running. Someone has to hold the paper the central bank has stopped buying, and Japanese households are being courted for the job. Improvements to retail JGB terms have been trailed; watch whether they actually arrive.</p>

<p>And watch the ratio rather than the headline. If the cash share keeps sliding while fund inflows flatten out, the money is just circulating inside the safe bucket — and "savings to investment" is a weaker signal than it looks.</p>
