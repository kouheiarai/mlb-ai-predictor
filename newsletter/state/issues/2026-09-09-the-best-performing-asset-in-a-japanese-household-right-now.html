The number that matters most to a Japanese household came out on Monday, and it is not a rate.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare published July's monthly labour survey. Average total cash earnings, at firms with five or more employees: ¥436,401, up 4.7% on the year. Real wages rose 2.4%, the seventh consecutive positive month.

Set that against the two numbers this newsletter has spent the past fortnight on.

What it grows at Annual
Wages, nominal 4.7%
Retail government bond, fixed 5-year 2.06%
Megabank ordinary deposit 0.40%

Those are not the same kind of number — one is a growth rate on income, the others are yields on a stock of money — and stacking them is a rhetorical move, not an analytical one. But the household experiences them in the same wallet, and the ordering is the point.

The money coming in is growing nearly twelve times faster than the money already sitting in the bank. For a Japanese household in 2026, the best-performing financial asset is the salary.

What is actually driving it

Before anyone gets carried away, the 4.7% is flattered. July is a bonus month, and bonuses did the heavy lifting:

Component July 2026 Year on year
Total cash earnings ¥436,401 +4.7%
— scheduled pay ¥280,797 +4.1%
— bonuses and special payments ¥134,819 +6.3%
Part-timers, hourly scheduled pay ¥1,460 +5.7%

Strip out the bonus and scheduled pay is still up 4.1%, which is the number to watch, because scheduled pay is what recurs. A bonus is a decision a company makes once a year. Base pay is a decision it has to keep making.

The last line is the one almost nobody outside Japan is looking at. Part-timers' hourly rate rose 5.7%. Full-time workers' scheduled pay rose 3.9%. Those are different units — an hourly rate against a monthly salary — so treat the comparison loosely. But the direction has been consistent for a while now, and it points at something structural: in a labour market this tight, the fastest raises are happening at the bottom.

Japan spent thirty years with a wage structure that barely moved. It is moving now, and it is moving fastest where the shortage bites hardest.

What the raise is worth

Put the two halves of the household balance sheet in the same unit and the gap stops being abstract.

Scheduled pay went from about ¥269,700 a year ago to ¥280,797 now. That is roughly ¥11,000 more a month, or about ¥133,000 over a year — on base pay alone, ignoring the bonus.

To earn ¥133,000 of interest in a megabank ordinary account at 0.40%, you would need to have about ¥33 million sitting in it.

So this year's pay rise, for an average worker, is worth what a fairly serious pile of savings earns. Not a life-changing sum either way — but it puts the two engines in proportion. For most Japanese households the salary is not merely the larger engine; it is the only one currently running.

Which inflation are we deflating by?

Here is a detail that changes the headline and that almost no English coverage mentions.

A real wage is a nominal wage divided by a price index — and Japan's labour ministry publishes the calculation twice, using two different indices:

  • Deflated by CPI excluding imputed rent: real wages +2.4% (that deflator ran +2.2%)
  • Deflated by headline CPI: real wages +2.6% (that deflator ran +2.0%)

The first is the one that gets reported. Imputed rent — the notional rent an owner-occupier is treated as paying themselves — is a large, slow-moving component of the headline index, and excluding it produces a higher inflation reading and therefore a lower real wage.

Neither is wrong. The ministry publishes both precisely because the choice is a judgement call. But it means that when you read "Japanese real wages rose 2.4%", you are reading one of two official answers, and the other one is two-tenths better.

A caveat on my own arithmetic: these are preliminary figures and get revised. And the deflator the ministry cites for headline CPI, +2.0%, is a tenth above what the Statistics Bureau published for July in its own release. I have not established why, so I have kept every calculation here inside the ministry's own numbers rather than mixing the two sources.

What it adds up to

Nominal wage growth has now held above 3% for six consecutive months — the first such run since March 1992, according to Nikkei.

That date should look familiar. Two weeks ago the megabank deposit rate hit a level it had not seen in about 34 years, which lands in the same year. Both series are described as "back to 1992". They are not back to 1992 in the same proportion, and the difference between them is the whole of this newsletter's subject.

Which makes the question this newsletter keeps circling more consequential every month, not less. When incomes were flat, where a household parked its money was close to irrelevant, because there was not much new money and nothing paid anything to hold it. Both halves of that have now changed. Income is growing at a rate Japan has not seen in a generation, and the destinations finally differ from each other by amounts you can notice.

Japan's households are being paid properly again, and the money they are paid still lands somewhere that does almost nothing with it. The flow is fixed. The stock is not.
