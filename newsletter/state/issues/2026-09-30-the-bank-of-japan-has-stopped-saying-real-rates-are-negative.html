On 18 September the Bank of Japan raised the uncollateralised overnight call rate from 1.0% to 1.25%, effective 24 September. The basic loan rate went from 1.25% to 1.5%. Every wire had it within minutes.

The quarter point is not the interesting part. Set the September statement beside June's and read them line by line, and the Bank has changed how it describes the world.

What changed in the statement

June 16 September 18
"Real interest rates have been negative, mainly in the short- to medium-term zone." "Real interest rates have remained at low levels, mainly in the short- to medium-term zone."
Overshoot risk attributed to "the price pass-through stemming from the rise in crude oil prices" Overshoot risk attributed to "firms' behavior shifting more toward raising wages and prices"
— New sentence: stabilising inflation around 2% "becomes important in order to keep the risk of… deviating upward… from materializing and thereby exerting an adverse impact on the economy afterward."
CPI ex fresh food "at a level below 2 percent"; the annex says mid-1% CPI ex fresh food "has been moderately rising"; the annex says high-1%

The deleted word

Start with the first row, because it is the one that will not be reported anywhere else.

For years the Bank's argument for why policy remained accommodative even as the nominal rate rose was that the real rate — nominal minus expected inflation — was still below zero. Negative real rates mean money is effectively free. It was the load-bearing sentence in the case for going slowly.

In September that sentence is gone. Real rates are now described as "at low levels". The Japanese original makes the same move: マイナス becomes なお低い水準.

Low is not negative. The Bank has not announced that policy has stopped being accommodative — it says in the next breath that accommodative conditions will be maintained. But it has quietly retired the strongest version of that claim, and it did so in the same statement in which it raised rates.

The cause of inflation was reassigned

In June the risk of overshooting 2% was blamed on oil: crude prices up, costs passing through business to business, possibly reaching consumers. That is imported inflation, and it is the kind that fades.

In September the same risk is attributed to "firms' behavior shifting more toward raising wages and prices." That is domestic, and it is the kind that does not fade, because it is a habit rather than a shock.

A central bank that thinks inflation is oil can wait. A central bank that thinks inflation is how companies now behave has less reason to.

The vote split wider

June was 7–1. September was 7–2.

Asada Toichiro dissented both times, but for different stated reasons. In June it was the Middle East: downside risks to production and employment outweighed upside risks to prices. In September he argued instead that CPI excluding fresh food is running below 2% and "it could not necessarily be said that the economic situation was strong."

The second dissent is the one to notice. Sato Ayano voted against at her first meeting on the board, on the grounds that economic and price developments had not accelerated enough to justify moving now.

One more detail from the attendance list, which nobody reads: Governor Ueda was absent from the June meeting, which Deputy Governor Himino chaired. He was back in the chair in September.

The market read it as dovish

Here is the part that looks wrong until you see why.

The Bank raised rates, and the yen fell — to the 158 level against the dollar on the evening of the 18th, according to Nikkei. The Nikkei 225 rose several hundred points.

Analysts quoted in the Japanese coverage attribute this to two things: the hike itself was fully priced in, and the two dissenting votes read as a signal that the next one is not close. On that reading, a rate rise with a widening split inside the board is less hawkish than a smaller move agreed unanimously.

I would add that the wording changes point the other way, and the market does not appear to have priced them at all.

What it means for a household

Three things follow, and this newsletter has the numbers for all of them.

Deposits. The megabank ordinary account went to 0.40% in August, set when the policy rate was 1.0%. At 1.25% there is room for another move. Whether the banks hand it over at the same fraction is the open question — they passed through well under half last time.

Retail government bonds. The rate on the retail JGB is a formula off the base rate, so it rises mechanically. The variable ten-year still pays two-thirds of it.

Mortgages. Three-quarters of Japan's recent borrowers are on variable rates. Most of them will notice nothing: the five-year rule freezes the monthly payment regardless. The second rate rise of 2026 will show up in their principal balance, not their bank statement, and not for years.

The Bank says it will "continue to raise the policy interest rate." Two of its nine members now disagree about the pace. Both of those facts are in the same document, and the household is on the receiving end of whichever one wins.
