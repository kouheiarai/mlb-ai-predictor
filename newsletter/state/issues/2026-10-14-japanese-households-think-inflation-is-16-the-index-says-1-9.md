# Japanese households think inflation is 16%. The index says 1.9%.

*The Bank of Japan asks them every quarter and publishes the answer. The gap explains more about Japanese saving than the statistics do.*

- 配信日: 2026-10-14
- 号種: weekly

---

## レビュー用サマリー（日本語・配信されません）

**この号の主張**

10月13日公表の日銀「生活意識に関するアンケート調査」（第107回・2026年9月調査）で、**家計が実感している過去1年の物価上昇率は平均＋16.2％、中央値＋10.0％。** 同時期の実際の全国CPI（総合）は＋1.9％。実感は実測の8.5倍ある。5年後の予想は平均＋10.1％（年率）、中央値＋5.0％で、日銀の目標2％の5倍と2.5倍。**この調査は、日銀が9月に利上げした際に根拠として挙げた「中長期の予想物価上昇率の上昇」の出どころでもある。** あわせて、現在の収入DIが▲5.4と郵送調査開始（2006年9月）以来の最高になった一方、家計が楽になったと感じていない理由を、この物価実感から説明する。1〜8号目で観測してきた家計の行動（0.4％の預金、2.06％の国債、＋4.7％の賃金への反応）も、この認識を通すと見え方が変わる。

**確信度が低い箇所**

- **「実感が実測を上回るのは世界共通の現象」という説明は、本稿の解釈であって出典を付けていない。** 食品や燃料のように購入頻度の高い品目が記憶に残りやすい、という一般的な説明を書いたが、研究を引用していない。本文でも断定を避け、筆者の読みとして書いている。日本の家計が特別に間違っている、という書き方には**していない**。
- 調査は統計調査ではなく世論調査である（日銀自身が冒頭でそう明記している）。本文でもその位置づけに触れた。
- 平均値は上下0.5％を除いたトリム平均。全サンプルの単純平均は＋16.9％で、本文では両方に触れている。
- CPIは8月分（9月18日公表）、調査は9月実施。**完全な同一時点の比較ではない。** ただしCPIは7月＋1.9％、8月＋1.9％と安定しており、比較の桁は変わらない。本文でも月を明記した。
- 収入D.I.の「2006年9月以来の最高」は、郵送調査に切り替わった2006年9月以降の系列における最高という意味。それ以前とは接続していない。本文でもその限定を書いた。
- 5年後の＋10.1％は**年率**（「毎年、平均何％程度変化すると思うか」）。累積ではない。読み違えやすいので本文で明示した。

**コンプライアンス自己点検**

- 個別銘柄・商品の推奨なし。預金・国債・賃金の数字は過去号の再掲で、比較のためだけに使っている。
- 物価や金利の予想なし。日銀の文言は9月声明からの引用として扱い、今後の政策には触れていない。
- **家計を無知だと描いていないか、通しで確認した。** 実感が実測を上回る理由を先に置き、「間違っている」ではなく「違うものを測っている」という書き方にしている。
- 日銀への評価も抑えた。「期待に基づいて利上げしている」という事実を並べただけで、政策の当否は書いていない。

## 本文（この HTML を beehiiv に貼る）

```html
<p>Every quarter the Bank of Japan asks a few thousand ordinary people a question no statistical agency asks: by how much do you think prices have changed over the past year?</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.boj.or.jp/research/o_survey/data/ishiki2610.pdf">September round came out on Monday</a>. Here is what Japan said.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Prices rose over the past year</strong> &mdash; said by <strong>95.5%</strong> of respondents</li>
  <li><strong>By how much, on average</strong> &mdash; <strong>+16.2%</strong> &middot; the untrimmed mean is +16.9%</li>
  <li><strong>By how much, median answer</strong> &mdash; <strong>+10.0%</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Answered "10.1% or more"</strong> &mdash; 44.3% of everyone asked</li>
</ul>

<p>The <a href="https://www.stat.go.jp/data/cpi/sokuhou/tsuki/pdf/zenkoku.pdf">consumer price index for August</a>, published by the Statistics Bureau three weeks before the survey closed, put inflation at <strong>1.9%</strong>.</p>

<p>Japanese households believe prices rose roughly <strong>eight and a half times</strong> as fast as the index says they did. Take the median instead of the mean, to strip out the extreme answers, and they still believe it was more than five times.</p>

<h2>Before anyone calls this ignorance</h2>

<p>It is not a Japanese defect, and I do not think it is a mistake in any useful sense. People notice the prices they meet often &mdash; rice, eggs, petrol, the lunch set &mdash; and barely register the ones they meet twice a decade. A basket weighted by how often you buy something looks nothing like a basket weighted by how much you spend, which is what the index measures.</p>

<p>So households are not getting the answer wrong. They are answering a different question, accurately. The trouble is what happens when policy treats the two answers as the same thing.</p>

<h2>It is not a few cranks</h2>

<p>The usual way to dismiss a survey number like 16.2% is to say the mean is being dragged by a handful of extreme answers. Normally that is a fair objection. Here it is not available.</p>

<p><strong>44.3% of respondents &mdash; nearly half the sample &mdash; answered that prices rose 10.1% or more.</strong> Another 30.1% put it between 5.1% and 10%. The tail is not a tail. It is most of the country.</p>

<p>And the Bank already trims: the published mean drops the top and bottom half-percent of answers. Without that trim it reads 16.9% rather than 16.2%, so the extremes are worth seven-tenths of a point in a figure sitting fourteen points above the index.</p>

<h2>They think the worst is over</h2>

<p>One more pair of numbers, because the direction matters as much as the level.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Prices over the past year</strong> &mdash; believed to be <strong>+16.2%</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Prices over the coming year</strong> &mdash; expected at <strong>+11.8%</strong> &middot; median +10.0%</li>
</ul>

<p>Households expect next year to be gentler than the year they think they just lived through. Both the one-year and five-year readings also fell from the June survey. The gap between what people believe and what the index measures is enormous, and it is closing.</p>

<h2>The awkward part</h2>

<p>This survey is not a curiosity. It is one of the places the Bank of Japan looks when it talks about inflation expectations &mdash; and inflation expectations are the reason it gives for raising rates.</p>

<p>From the <a href="https://www.boj.or.jp/mopo/mpmdeci/mpr_2026/k260918a.pdf">September statement</a>, which this newsletter went through line by line two weeks ago: there is a risk of inflation overshooting 2% "given factors such as firms' behavior shifting more toward raising wages and prices and <em>medium- to long-term inflation expectations rising</em>."</p>

<p>Here is what medium- to long-term looks like in the Bank's own survey. The question asks for the average <em>annual</em> rate over the coming five years, not the cumulative change:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Five years ahead, mean</strong> &mdash; <strong>+10.1%</strong> a year &middot; was +10.8% last quarter</li>
  <li><strong>Five years ahead, median</strong> &mdash; <strong>+5.0%</strong> a year</li>
  <li><strong>Expecting any rise at all</strong> &mdash; 86.5%</li>
  <li><strong>Answered "10.1% or more" for five years running</strong> &mdash; 22.6%</li>
</ul>

<p>The target is 2%. The median household expects two and a half times that, every year, for five years. The mean expects five times.</p>

<p>Both numbers moved <em>down</em> this quarter, which is the part the headlines missed.</p>

<h2>Which is why the pay rise does not land</h2>

<p>The same survey carries a number that should be cause for celebration.</p>

<p>The income diffusion index &mdash; the share saying their income rose minus the share saying it fell &mdash; came in at <strong>&minus;5.4</strong>, from &minus;10.7 three months earlier. The Bank's own footnote records it as the highest reading since the survey went to post in September 2006. Twenty years, and Japanese households have never felt better about their pay than they do now.</p>

<p>They still do not feel well off. This newsletter has been circling that contradiction for weeks: nominal wages up 4.7%, real wages positive for seven straight months, and a population that does not act as though any of it happened.</p>

<p>Put the two survey answers side by side and it stops being a contradiction. If you believe prices rose 16% and your pay rose 5%, you have had a large pay cut. The arithmetic in your head is wrong by the index's lights and completely coherent by your own.</p>

<h2>What it explains</h2>

<p>Run the past two months of this newsletter back through that lens.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The megabank deposit at 0.40%</strong> &mdash; against measured inflation that is a loss of 1.5 points a year &middot; against perceived inflation it is a catastrophe</li>
  <li><strong>The retail government bond at 2.06%</strong> &mdash; the first safe yen asset in a generation to clear the index &middot; and nowhere near clearing what people believe</li>
  <li><strong>Three-quarters of borrowers taking a variable mortgage</strong> &mdash; rational if you expect inflation to erode the debt faster than the rate rises</li>
</ul>

<p>Every one of those choices looks more sensible, not less, once you know what the chooser thinks inflation is.</p>

<p>The Bank of Japan has a 2% target, an index printing 1.9%, and a public that thinks the number is 16. It publishes all three itself, every quarter, and the third one is the only one nobody reports.</p>
```
